Glossary

Module 1

Economic Thinking

You will learn to

  • Explain scarcity and name the four factors of production
  • Describe how specialization and the division of labor lead to trade
  • Tell microeconomics from macroeconomics
  • Use economic models, and read slopes on graphs

Economics is the study of how people choose to use resources when those resources are scarce.

Scarcity

Scarcity means resources are limited relative to what people need and want. Time, money, labor, tools, land and raw materials are all finite. Even huge totals are not unlimited: in 2016 the U.S. labor force had more than 158 million workers, and the country covers about 3.8 million square miles. Because resources are limited, the goods and services we can produce are limited too, while human wants keep growing.

Choices under scarcity happen at every level. A student can't take every class with the best professor at the ideal time. A family picks between a new car and a vacation. A town weighs police protection against schools, and a nation weighs defense against environmental protection.

Economic (scarce) goodsIf they were free, people would want more than exists, so they carry a positive price. Most goods are like this.
Free goodsSupply exceeds demand even at a price of zero, like sand in a desert. Clean air used to be free; it is increasingly scarce.

The factors of production

FactorWhat it includes

CapitalAnything made to produce other goods: tools, machinery, buildings. Money (financial capital) is not itself productive, but it can buy capital.
LaborAny human effort, physical or mental (also called human capital)
EntrepreneurshipSpotting a profit opportunity, organizing the other factors, and taking the risk

Specialization, division of labor and trade

Adam Smith's The Wealth of Nations (1776) opens with a pin factory. Making a pin took about 18 separate tasks. One worker doing everything might make 20 pins a day, but a team of 10 workers, each doing two or three tasks, made about 48,000. That is the power of the division of labor and specialization.

Specialization raises output for three reasons: people differ in skills and talents (and places differ in climate and resources); workers get faster and better through repetition; and large operations enjoy economies of scale, where average cost falls as output grows (a plant making 50,000 cars a year makes each car far more cheaply than one making 100). But specialization only works if people can trade: you earn income doing one thing and use it to buy everything else. Markets make that possible.

Microeconomics and macroeconomics

Microeconomics studies individual households, workers and firms and how they interact in particular markets. Macroeconomics looks at the economy as a whole: growth, unemployment, inflation, and government budgets and trade. This book is mainly about micro, with a look at international trade and finance at the end.

Economic models

An economic model is a simplified version of reality that helps us understand and predict behavior. A good model is simple enough to grasp but captures what matters. Models can be expressed in words, graphs or algebra. Economists use graphs to discover answers, not just to illustrate them.

The circular flow diagram is a basic model: households buy goods and services from firms in the product market, and sell their labor to firms in the labor market. Money flows one way and goods, services and labor flow the other.

Reading graphs: slope

The slope of a line is rise over run: the change on the vertical axis divided by the change on the horizontal axis. A positive slope means the variables move together (price and quantity supplied). A negative slope means they move in opposite directions (price and quantity demanded). A slope of zero is a flat line: no relationship.

Worked example. A line passes through (2, 1) and (6, 3). Rise = 3 − 1 = 2, run = 6 − 2 = 4, so the slope is 2/4 = ½.

Curves have slopes too, measured between two points. In the table below, total cost is Q²: it gets steeper as output rises.

Key takeaways

  • Scarcity (limited resources, unlimited wants) forces choices.
  • Specialization and the division of labor multiply output, and they require trade.
  • Models simplify reality; slope = rise ÷ run tells us how two variables are related.
Module 2

Choice in a World of Scarcity

You will learn to

  • Draw a budget constraint and calculate opportunity cost
  • Ignore sunk costs
  • Use the PPF to show trade-offs, efficiency and diminishing returns
  • Apply marginal analysis, and separate positive from normative statements

Every choice has a cost: whatever you give up to get it.

Budget constraints

A budget constraint shows every combination of goods a person can afford with their income (or time), assuming they spend all of it. Points on the line use the whole budget, points inside leave money unspent, and points outside are unaffordable.

Charlie's budget. Charlie has $10 a week for burgers ($2 each) and bus tickets ($0.50 each). He could buy 5 burgers and no tickets, 20 tickets and no burgers, or a mix such as 2 burgers ($4) and 12 tickets ($6). Each extra burger costs him $2 ÷ $0.50 = 4 bus tickets. That is its opportunity cost, and it is also the slope of his budget line.
Lab 2.1Charlie's budget constraint

Change income and prices, then choose how many burgers to buy. The rest of the budget goes on bus tickets.

Sunk costs

Sunk costs are past costs that can't be recovered, and they shouldn't affect decisions about the future. If you've paid $8 for a movie and it's awful after 30 minutes, the $8 is gone either way; the real question is whether the remaining 90 minutes are worth more than anything else you could do. Firms make the same mistake when they keep a failing product alive because of past development spending.

The production possibilities frontier

A society faces a similar constraint. A production possibilities frontier (PPF) shows the combinations of two goods an economy can produce with its resources and technology. Picture health care and education: with 10 teachers each teaching 25 students, the most education possible is 250 students.

Budget constraintPPF
ShowsWhat a person can buyWhat a society can produce
ShapeStraight lineUsually bowed outward
SlopeFixed price ratioChanging opportunity cost

Why the bow? Resources are specialized. Moving teachers out of health care into schools costs little health care and adds a lot of education, so the curve starts flat. But to push education further you must move doctors into classrooms, giving up a lot of health care for little extra education, so the curve gets steep. This is the law of diminishing returns: each extra increment of resources devoted to one goal adds less than the last.

Productive and allocative efficiency

Productive efficiency means you can't produce more of one good without producing less of another: every point on the PPF qualifies, and points inside waste resources. Allocative efficiency means producing the mix society most wants, which is one particular point on the frontier. A young society might choose more education; an older one, more health care. An economy can get more of everything only by becoming more efficient (moving to the frontier) or by growing (shifting the frontier out).

Different countries have different PPFs. Brazil grows a lot of sugar cane per acre, the U.S. a lot of wheat. Each has a lower opportunity cost, a comparative advantage, in one crop, so both gain by specializing and trading (Module 14).

Thinking at the margin

Most choices are about a little more or a little less. Marginal cost is the change in total cost from a choice; marginal benefit is the change in what you receive. A one-week beach rental costs $2,000 and two weeks $3,600, so the second week's marginal cost is only $1,600. A rational choice is one where MB > MC: if a full gym membership costs $10 a month more than basic and adds the weight room, it's worth it only if the weight room is worth $10 a month to you.

Positive and normative statements

A positive statement describes the world as it is and can be tested ("a higher minimum wage reduces teen employment"). A normative statement says how things should be ("the minimum wage should be raised"). Economists can test the first kind; the second depends on values.

Key takeaways

  • Budget constraints and PPFs show trade-offs; their slopes measure opportunity cost.
  • Ignore sunk costs; decide at the margin, choosing when MB > MC.
  • The PPF bows out because of diminishing returns; points on it are productively efficient.
Module 3

Supply and Demand

You will learn to

  • Compare market, command and mixed economies
  • List what shifts demand and what shifts supply
  • Find equilibrium from a table, a graph or algebra
  • Predict what happens when one or both curves shift

Supply and demand is the most widely used model in economics. It explains prices in almost any market.

Economic systems

Every economy must answer three questions: what to produce, how to produce it, and who gets it. In a market economy, private owners make these decisions through markets, guided by profit and consumer demand (Hong Kong, Singapore, the U.S.). A market is competitive when there are so many buyers and sellers that none controls the price, and free when government doesn't set prices. In a command economy the government owns resources and decides what is made and at what price (today, essentially North Korea and Cuba). Most real economies are mixed: the U.S. sits near the market end, many European countries a bit further toward government involvement, and China and Russia closer to command.

Demand

The quantity demanded is what buyers will purchase at a given price. The law of demand says it falls as price rises. A change in the good's own price is a movement along the demand curve. Anything else shifts the curve, ceteris paribus (all else equal).

Cars and income. At $20,000, buyers want 18 million cars. If incomes rise, demand shifts right and they want 20 million at that price; if incomes fall, 14.4 million. The shift happens at every price, not just $20,000.
Demand shifterIncreases demand when…
TastesThe good becomes more popular (U.S. chicken consumption rose from 33 to 81 lb per person, 1980–2012, while beef fell from 77 to 57)
PopulationThere are more buyers (an aging population raises demand for hearing aids)
IncomeIncome rises, for a normal good. For an inferior good (generic groceries, used cars) demand falls when income rises
Price of a substituteThe substitute gets more expensive (cheaper tablets cut demand for laptops)
Price of a complementThe complement gets cheaper (cheaper golf clubs raise demand for golf balls)
ExpectationsBuyers expect higher prices or shortages later (stocking up before a hurricane)

Supply

Firms supply more when it's more profitable. Anything that lowers production costs shifts supply right; anything that raises them shifts it left.

If cheese rises by $0.75 per pizza, the pizzeria needs $0.75 more for every pizza: the supply curve shifts up (left) by exactly the cost increase.

Solving with algebra

If demand is Qd = 16 − 2P and supply is Qs = 2 + 5P, set them equal: 16 − 2P = 2 + 5P, so 14 = 7P and P = $2. Plug back in: Qd = 16 − 4 = 12 and Qs = 2 + 10 = 12, so Q = 12.

Four steps for analyzing changes

  1. Draw the original supply and demand and mark equilibrium.
  2. Decide which curve the event affects.
  3. Decide which way it shifts.
  4. Compare the new equilibrium with the old.

Take the U.S. Postal Service. A pay raise for postal workers raises costs, so supply shifts left: price up, quantity down. Email and texting reduce demand: price down, quantity down. Together, quantity definitely falls, but the price could go either way, depending on which shift is bigger. Remember the distinction: a change in demand shifts the curve; a change in quantity demanded is a movement along it, caused by a supply shift.

Key takeaways

  • Own-price changes move along curves; other factors shift them.
  • Equilibrium is where Qd = Qs; surpluses push prices down and shortages push them up.
  • When both curves shift, you can usually predict only price or quantity.
Module 4

Applications of Supply and Demand

You will learn to

  • Analyze price ceilings and price floors
  • Measure consumer, producer and social surplus
  • Explain why price controls cause deadweight loss
  • Apply supply and demand to labor and financial markets

Governments sometimes decide the market price is "wrong" and set it by law. Supply and demand predicts what happens next.

Price ceilings

A price ceiling is a legal maximum price, such as rent control. It's binding when set below equilibrium, and then it causes a shortage.

Rent control. Apartments start at $500 with 15,000 units rented. Demand rises, so the market rent would climb to $600 with 17,000 units. A $500 ceiling keeps rents down, but at $500 renters want 19,000 apartments and landlords supply only 15,000: a shortage of 4,000. Fewer apartments are rented than without the ceiling.

Binding ceilings also lower quality (landlords skimp on maintenance), shrink supply (rentals converted to condos), and benefit only the buyers lucky enough to get the good.

Price floors

A price floor is a legal minimum price. Set above equilibrium it causes a surplus. Farm price supports are typical: governments often buy the surplus, so farmers gain while taxpayers and consumers pay. The EU's Common Agricultural Policy cost about €60 billion a year in 2014–2020, about 38% of the EU budget. The minimum wage is a price floor in the labor market ($7.25 an hour federally in 2016); critics argue it creates a labor surplus (unemployment). Note that price controls don't shift the curves; they move the market along them.

Consumer and producer surplus

The height of the demand curve is the most buyers would pay; the height of the supply curve is the least sellers would accept. Consumer surplus is the gap between willingness to pay and the price, the area below demand and above price. Producer surplus is the area above supply and below price. Together they make social surplus, which is largest at equilibrium.

Worked example. Equilibrium is $10 and 3 units; the demand curve starts at $25 and supply at $5. Consumer surplus = ½ × 3 × ($25 − $10) = $22.50. Producer surplus = ½ × 3 × ($10 − $5) = $7.50. Social surplus = $30.

Why price controls are inefficient

Ceilings and floors block trades that buyers and sellers both wanted. They transfer surplus (a ceiling moves some producer surplus to consumers; a floor does the reverse), but they also destroy some: that loss is the deadweight loss, value no one gets. That's why consumers lobby for ceilings and producers for floors, even though society as a whole loses.

Labor and financial markets

Supply and demand work in any market. In the labor market, workers supply labor and employers demand it; the "price" is the wage. In financial markets, savers supply funds and borrowers demand them; the price is the interest rate.

New information technology substitutes for low-skill workers (file clerks) but complements high-skill ones (managers). Demand for low-skill labor shifts left (lower wages) and for high-skill labor right (higher wages). The college wage premium rose from about 30% in 1980 to about 60% in 2012. In finance, if foreign investors grow wary of U.S. debt, the supply of capital shifts left: interest rates rise and less is borrowed.

Key takeaways

  • Binding ceilings cause shortages; binding floors cause surpluses.
  • Social surplus = consumer + producer surplus, maximized at equilibrium.
  • Price controls transfer surplus and create deadweight loss.

Elasticity

You will learn to

  • Calculate price elasticity with the midpoint formula
  • Classify demand and supply as elastic, inelastic or unit elastic
  • Use income and cross-price elasticities
  • Predict effects on total revenue and on who pays a tax

Elasticity measures how strongly one variable responds to a change in another, in percentage terms.

Price elasticity of demand

Price elasticity of demand = % change in quantity demanded ÷ % change in price

The midpoint method uses the average of the two values as the base, so you get the same answer whether the price rises or falls:

%ΔQ = (Q₂ − Q₁) ÷ [(Q₂ + Q₁)/2] × 100    %ΔP = (P₂ − P₁) ÷ [(P₂ + P₁)/2] × 100
Two points on one demand curve. From $70 (2,800 units) to $60 (3,000 units): %ΔQ = 200/2,900 = 6.9% and %ΔP = −10/65 = −15.4%, so elasticity ≈ 0.45: inelastic. From $120 (1,800) to $130 (1,600): %ΔQ = −11.8% and %ΔP = 8.0%, so elasticity ≈ 1.47: elastic. Same straight line, same slope, different elasticity: demand gets more elastic higher up the curve.

Total revenue = price × quantity. If demand is inelastic, raising the price raises revenue; if elastic, raising the price lowers it.

Price elasticity of supply

Supply elasticity uses the same formula with quantity supplied, and it's always positive. If apartments renting at $650 bring 10,000 units onto the market and $700 brings 13,000, %ΔQ = 26.1% and %ΔP = 7.4%, so supply elasticity ≈ 3.5 (elastic).

Widgets and sprockets. Widgets fall from $12 to $9 and sales rise from 8 to 10 (own-price elasticity ≈ −0.77). Sprocket sales rise from 9 to 10, a 10.5% rise, against a −28.6% change in the widget price: cross-price elasticity ≈ −0.37, so they are complements.

Tax incidence

Who really pays a tax doesn't depend on who hands the money to the government. The burden falls mostly on the less elastic side of the market, the side that can't easily walk away. Smokers bear most of cigarette taxes (inelastic demand); owners of beachfront hotels bear much of a hotel tax (inelastic supply). And when both sides are elastic, a tax shrinks the market so much that it raises little revenue.

Key takeaways

  • Use the midpoint formula; report elasticity as an absolute value.
  • Inelastic demand: price and revenue move together. Elastic: they move apart.
  • The less elastic side of the market bears more of a tax.

Utility

You will learn to

  • Compute total and marginal utility
  • Find consumer equilibrium using marginal utility per dollar
  • Explain income and substitution effects

Behind every demand curve is a person trying to get the most satisfaction from a limited budget.

Total and marginal utility

Utility is the satisfaction people get from consuming. Total utility is the satisfaction from everything consumed; marginal utility (MU) is the change from one more unit. Typically each extra unit adds less than the one before: diminishing marginal utility. Total utility still rises, just more slowly. (Utils are a teaching device; all that matters is that people can rank what they prefer.)

Maximizing utility

José has $56. T-shirts cost $14 and movies $7. His affordable bundles run from 4 T-shirts and no movies to 8 movies and no T-shirts. The best bundle, his consumer equilibrium, is the one with the highest total utility. The rule that finds it: keep buying whichever good gives the most marginal utility per dollar (MU ÷ price) until the budget runs out. At the optimum:

When income or prices change

Jazmin spends $1,000 a year on concerts ($50) and bed-and-breakfast getaways ($200). She chooses 8 concerts and 3 getaways. If her income doubles, she might buy more of both (most common), or more of one and less of the other. If she buys less of something as income rises, it's an inferior good for her.

A price rise has two effects. The substitution effect: the good is now relatively expensive, so you buy less of it and more of other things. The income effect: your income buys less overall, so (for normal goods) you buy less of everything. For the good whose price rose, both effects cut consumption. For other goods they pull in opposite directions.

Indifference curves

An indifference curve joins bundles that give equal utility. Curves slope down, bow toward the origin, and higher is better. Their slope is the marginal rate of substitution (MRS): how much of one good you'd give up for one more of the other. Lilly would give up 36 doughnuts for a third book when she has few books, but only 5 for a twelfth: the MRS falls because of diminishing marginal utility. Her best choice is where the highest reachable indifference curve just touches her budget line, where MRS equals the price ratio.

Behavioral economics

Traditional economics assumes people are fully rational. Behavioral economics adds psychology:

  • Loss aversion: losing $1 hurts about 2.25 times as much as gaining $1 pleases.
  • Mental accounting: people treat "found" money differently from earned money, even though money is fungible. Someone with $1,000 of credit card debt at 15% and $2,000 of savings at 2% loses about $130 a year by not paying the debt off.
  • Self-control and nudges: automatic enrollment in retirement plans (with an opt-out) raises participation to nearly everyone.
  • Bounded rationality: limited time, information and attention keep us from always finding the best choice.

Key takeaways

  • Marginal utility diminishes; total utility is the sum of marginal utilities.
  • Consumer equilibrium equalizes MU per dollar across goods (MRS = price ratio).
  • Price changes work through substitution and income effects.
Module 7

Production and Costs

You will learn to

  • Use a production function and explain diminishing marginal product
  • Calculate accounting and economic profit
  • Compute and graph average and marginal costs
  • Explain economies and diseconomies of scale

To understand supply, we need to know what it costs a firm to produce.

The production function

A production function shows the most output a firm can make from its inputs, Q = f(L, K). Fixed inputs (a building under lease) can't change quickly; variable inputs (ingredients, staff) can. In the short run at least one input is fixed; in the long run all inputs can change.

Marginal product is the extra output from one more worker. With a two-person crosscut saw, 1 lumberjack cuts 4 trees, 2 cut 10, 3 cut 12, 4 cut 13 and 5 still cut 13. Marginal product rises (4, then 6) and then falls (2, 1, 0): the law of diminishing marginal product. Extra workers must share fixed equipment.

Lab 7.1A typing firm: short run vs long run

Add typists. With one PC, diminishing returns set in at the second worker. Give the firm three PCs (the long-run choice) and they start later.

Costs and profit

Explicit costs are actual payments (wages, rent, materials). Implicit costs are opportunity costs of resources the firm already owns, like the owner's time.

Accounting profit = Revenue − Explicit costs   |   Economic profit = Revenue − Explicit − Implicit costs
Fred's law practice. Fred earns $125,000 at a corporate firm. On his own he'd take in $200,000 and pay $50,000 rent and $35,000 for a clerk. Accounting profit is $115,000, but subtracting his forgone salary gives an economic profit of −$10,000.

Total cost = fixed cost + variable cost. Per unit: ATC = TC ÷ Q, AVC = VC ÷ Q, AFC = FC ÷ Q and MC = ΔTC ÷ ΔQ. Here is a barbershop, The Clip Joint, with $160 of fixed costs and $80 per worker:

The long run and economies of scale

In the long run a firm can choose its plant size. The long-run average cost (LRAC) curve traces the lowest average cost for each output. When LRAC falls as output grows, there are economies of scale: a small factory makes 1,000 alarm clocks at $12 each, a medium one 2,000 at $8, and a large one 5,000 at $4. Doubling a pipe's circumference roughly quadruples its capacity; chemical engineers' "six-tenths rule" says raising output by some percentage raises cost by only about 60% of that percentage.

Typically LRAC falls (economies of scale), flattens (constant returns to scale), then rises (diseconomies of scale), as huge organizations grow hard to manage. Don't confuse this with diminishing marginal returns, which is a short-run idea with some inputs fixed.

Key takeaways

  • Diminishing marginal product makes marginal cost rise.
  • Economic profit subtracts implicit as well as explicit costs.
  • MC crosses ATC and AVC at their minimums; LRAC shows economies and diseconomies of scale.

Perfect Competition

You will learn to

  • Describe a perfectly competitive market
  • Find profit-maximizing output with totals and with marginals
  • Apply the break-even and shutdown rules
  • Explain entry, exit and long-run efficiency

In perfect competition, no single firm can change the price. Its only real decision is how much to produce.

The price taker

Perfect competition has many firms selling identical products, with free entry and exit. Each firm is a price taker facing a horizontal demand curve at the market price, so each extra unit adds exactly the price to revenue. Memory aid "MR. DARP": Marginal Revenue = Demand = Average Revenue = Price.

Profit-maximizing rule: produce where MR = MC  →  for a price taker, P = MC

Break-even and shutdown

The break-even point is where MC crosses ATC at its minimum: at that price, economic profit is zero. The shutdown point is where MC crosses AVC at its minimum. Fixed costs must be paid either way in the short run, so a firm keeps operating at a loss as long as revenue covers variable costs.

Yoga studio. Rent is $10,000 a month (fixed); instructors cost $15,000 (variable). With $20,000 of revenue it loses $5,000 by staying open but $10,000 by closing: stay open. With $12,000 of revenue it can't even cover instructors, so it should close and lose only the $10,000 rent.

The firm's short-run supply curve is its MC curve above minimum AVC.

Entry, exit and the long run

Profits attract entry: new firms shift market supply right and push the price down. Losses cause exit, which pushes the price up. This stops only when price equals the minimum of average cost, at zero economic profit (a "normal" return that covers all opportunity costs). Exit is common: in 2011, about 535,000 U.S. firms started and 576,000 failed.

Efficiency

In long-run equilibrium, perfect competition is productively efficient (goods are made at the lowest average cost) and allocatively efficient (P = MC, so the value of the last unit to buyers equals its cost to society). If P > MC, society would gain from more output; if P < MC, from less. "Efficient" is a technical term: it doesn't address pollution, poverty or unequal ability to pay.

Key takeaways

  • Produce where P = MR = MC.
  • Operate if P ≥ min AVC; shut down below it; break even at min ATC.
  • Entry and exit drive long-run economic profit to zero, giving productive and allocative efficiency.

A monopoly sells all or nearly all of a product with no close substitutes, protected by barriers to entry.

A monopolist faces the downward-sloping market demand curve. To sell more it must cut the price on every unit, so marginal revenue is less than price. For a straight-line demand curve, MR starts at the same intercept and falls twice as fast. The monopolist produces where MR = MC, then reads the price off the demand curve.

Inefficiency and price discrimination

A single-price monopoly stops at a quantity where buyers still value extra units above their cost: the lost surplus is deadweight loss. Antitrust and mergers

U.S. antitrust law began with the Sherman Act (1890; Standard Oil was split into 34 firms in 1911), followed by the Clayton Act (1914), which banned mergers that "substantially lessen competition", and the creation of the FTC. Regulators measure concentration in two ways:

  • The four-firm concentration ratio: the market shares of the four largest firms added up.
  • The Herfindahl-Hirschman Index (HHI): the sum of every firm's squared market share. A monopoly scores 10,000; 100 equal firms score 100. Older FTC guidelines: below 1,000 probably approve, 1,000–1,800 look closely, above 1,800 probably challenge.

Regulating natural monopolies

Breaking up a natural monopoly raises average costs. Forcing price = MC is allocatively efficient but leaves the firm losing money (it would need a subsidy). Setting price = average cost lets it cover costs with a normal profit, the most practical choice. Cost-plus regulation does this but gives weak incentives to cut costs; price cap regulation fixes a maximum price for several years, so firms that cut costs keep the savings.

Key takeaways

  • Barriers to entry sustain monopoly; MR < P for a monopolist.
  • Produce where MR = MC and price from demand: higher price, less output, deadweight loss.
  • Antitrust uses concentration ratios and the HHI; regulators often set P = AC for natural monopolies.
Module 10

Monopolistic Competition and Oligopoly

You will learn to

  • Explain product differentiation and the monopolistic competitor's demand curve
  • Show why long-run profit falls to zero with free entry
  • Explain why oligopolies form and why collusion is unstable
  • Analyze the prisoner's dilemma and Nash equilibrium

Most real markets sit between the extremes of perfect competition and monopoly.

Monopolistic competition

Monopolistic competition means many firms selling differentiated products: restaurants, boutiques, beer, golf balls. Products differ in physical features, location, service (guarantees, delivery) and buyer perception (brand, advertising). Over 1,800 golf ball models meet official standards, and most players can't tell them apart, yet makers spend heavily marketing differences.

Each firm faces a downward-sloping but fairly elastic demand curve: raise the price and you lose some customers, not all. It maximizes profit where MR = MC. But barriers to entry are low, so short-run profits attract rivals, each of which shifts the firm's demand left until price equals average cost and economic profit is zero. Unlike perfect competition, the firm ends up with price above MC and above minimum average cost: not productively or allocatively efficient, but offering variety.

Lab 10.1Entry erodes a restaurant's profit

A restaurant with a new sauce earns profits. Let rivals enter one by one and watch its demand curve shift left until it just touches average cost.

Advertising tries to make demand less elastic or shift it right. U.S. advertising spending was about $139.5 billion in 2012. Critics see much of it as wasteful, rival ads canceling each other out; defenders value variety and information.

Oligopoly

An oligopoly has a few large firms, often because of economies of scale or other barriers. Each firm's best choice depends on what the others do. Firms can collude, acting together like a monopoly (a formal agreement is a cartel), or compete hard. Collusion is illegal under U.S. antitrust law: French detergent makers were fined €361 million, and lysine producers including ADM were fined $100 million in 1996.

The prisoner's dilemma

Game theory analyzes such situations. In the classic prisoner's dilemma, confessing is each prisoner's dominant strategy, so both confess and serve 5 years, though both staying silent would mean only 2 years each. In a duopoly, if both firms hold output low they earn $1,000 each; if one expands it gets $1,500 and the other $200; if both expand, $400 each. Expanding is dominant, so they end up at $400: a Nash equilibrium where neither can gain by changing alone, yet both are worse off.

How do firms sustain cooperation without legal contracts? By monitoring each other and punishing cheating. A kinked demand curve arises if rivals match price cuts but not increases, which discourages both. In repeated play, strategies like tit-for-tat (cooperate, then copy your rival's last move) can keep cooperation going.

8 rounds. Both hold output low: $1,000 each. Both expand: $400 each. If you expand while your rival holds low, you get $1,500 and they get $200 (and the reverse).

Key takeaways

  • Monopolistic competitors differentiate; entry drives long-run profit to zero.
  • Oligopolists are interdependent; collusion pays but is illegal and unstable.
  • In a prisoner's dilemma, dominant strategies lead to a Nash equilibrium that's worse for everyone.
Module 11

Public Goods and Externalities

You will learn to

  • Explain negative and positive externalities and why they cause market failure
  • Identify public goods, common resources and the free-rider problem
  • Compare command-and-control regulation with market-oriented tools
  • Apply the Coase theorem

Markets work well when buyers and sellers bear all the costs and benefits of their choices. Often they don't.

Negative externalities

A negative externality is a cost that falls on people outside the transaction, like pollution. Social cost = private cost + external cost. Because firms ignore the external part, the market produces too much: a market failure.

Refrigerators. Suppose each refrigerator causes $100 of pollution damage. If producers had to pay it, supply would shift up by $100. Equilibrium would move from $650 and 45,000 units to $700 and 40,000 units: a higher price, less output and less pollution.
Lab 11.1Pollution and the Pigouvian tax

Raise the external cost per unit. Then switch on a tax equal to it.

Positive externalities

With a positive externality, others benefit too: flu shots protect people who weren't vaccinated, and research spills over to competitors. Marginal social benefit is above marginal private benefit, so the market produces too little. Inventors capture only about one-third to one-half of an innovation's total benefits. If a drug company that can borrow at 8% counts only its private return, it invests $30 million in R&D; counting social benefits, the ideal is $52 million. Subsidies, vouchers and public funding of education and research narrow the gap. Education's private return is large (median weekly earnings in 2016: $519 without high school, $698 with a diploma, $1,270 with a bachelor's degree), and it also brings social benefits such as better health and lower crime.

Public goods and common resources

Private goodsRival & excludable: a pizza
Public goodsNon-rival & non-excludable: national defense, police and fire protection
Common resourcesRival & non-excludable: queen conch in Caribbean waters, ocean fish
Club goodsNon-rival & excludable: a patented invention while the patent lasts

People can enjoy a public good without paying for it, so markets underprovide it: the free-rider problem. Common resources suffer the tragedy of the commons (Garrett Hardin, 1968): nobody owns them, so nobody conserves them. Fishing licenses, catch limits, shorter seasons and outright bans (the U.S. has effectively banned conch harvesting since 1986) are the usual responses. Every public good has positive externalities, but not every good with positive externalities is a public good: students capture most of education's benefits.

Command-and-control regulation

The EPA (1970), the Clean Air Act (1970) and the Clean Water Act (1972) set effluent standards: legal limits on emissions, sometimes with required equipment. They've cleaned the air and water substantially, but critics note three weaknesses: no reward for beating the standard, the same rule for firms with very different costs of cutting pollution, and political loopholes.

Market-oriented tools

A pollution charge taxes each unit emitted. A firm cuts emissions wherever cutting is cheaper than paying the tax, so cleanup happens where it costs least.

Lab 11.2Pay the charge or clean up?

A firm emits 50 lbs of particulates. Cutting each 10 lbs costs $300, $500, $900, $1,500 and $2,500. Set the charge per 10 lbs and the firm picks the cheapest response.

Marketable permits cap total pollution and let firms trade allowances. Suppose four firms emitting 1,200 tons of lead get permits for half. Gamma cuts cheaply from 600 to 200 tons and sells 100 spare permits; Alpha (150 tons, 100 permits) and new entrant Delta (50 tons) buy 50 each. Total emissions hit the 600-ton cap, and the cheapest cuts get made first. The 1990 Clean Air Act amendments used shrinkable permits to halve sulfur dioxide from power plants. Permits suit a few hundred large emitters; charges suit millions of small ones (cars, cans); outright standards suit catastrophic dangers like nuclear waste.

Property rights and the Coase theorem

Sparks from a train set a farmer's field on fire: should the farmer build a fence or the railroad fit a spark catcher? Ronald Coase (Nobel 1991) showed that once property rights are clearly assigned and bargaining is cheap, the parties will find the cheapest fix themselves. The Coase theorem also explains why paying landowners to keep habitat works better than penalties that tempt them to hide endangered species.

Key takeaways

  • Negative externalities mean overproduction; positive ones mean underproduction.
  • Public goods invite free riders; common resources get overused.
  • Pollution charges and tradable permits reach a target at lower cost than uniform standards.

Labor Markets

You will learn to

  • Derive labor demand from marginal revenue product
  • Explain monopsony and why it lowers wages and employment
  • Analyze unions and bilateral monopoly

Labor is a market like any other, but it's a market for people's time, skills and livelihoods.

The demand for labor

Labor demand is a derived demand: firms want workers because they want what workers produce. A worker's marginal revenue product (MRP) is their marginal product times the marginal revenue from selling it. A firm hires until the wage equals MRP. In a perfectly competitive output market, MR = price, and MRP equals the value of the marginal product (MPL × P). Firms with market power have MR < P, so they demand less labor.

Unions

A labor union bargains collectively, acting like a monopoly seller of labor. It can push wages above equilibrium, which reduces employment (often through slower hiring or automation rather than layoffs), though higher morale and lower turnover may raise productivity. In 2016 about 10.7% of U.S. workers were union members (40.3% in local government but only 6.6% in the private sector), and members earned about 20% more than comparable nonunion workers. Membership has fallen since the mid-1950s, when about a quarter of workers belonged, because of the shift to services, import competition, protective laws and a tougher legal environment. When a union faces a monopsony (bilateral monopoly), employment falls below the competitive level and the wage depends on bargaining power.

Discrimination

Labor market discrimination means equally qualified workers get different pay or opportunities because of race or gender. Raw gaps don't prove it: comparisons must control for education and experience. The female-to-male earnings ratio reached about 80% after 2010, but controlling for education, experience and occupation, a 2007 study found a gap of about 5%. Mothers earn 7–14% less than comparable women without children. Black men earned 62% of white men's pay in 1964 and 75.3% in 2013, and résumés with white-sounding names got 50% more callbacks. Gary Becker argued competition punishes discriminating employers, yet discrimination has persisted. Key laws include the Equal Pay Act (1963) and the Civil Rights Act (1964).

Immigration

The U.S. received 10.1 million immigrants in 2000–2008, about half from the rest of the Americas. Most economists find immigration's overall gains real but modest: low-skill immigration lowers low-skill wages a little (around 1% in one study), helps buyers of those services, adds to federal tax revenue, and can strain state and local budgets. Policy proposals range from points systems favoring skills (Canada, Australia) to paths to citizenship for people brought to the U.S. as children.

Key takeaways

  • Firms hire until the wage equals marginal revenue product.
  • Monopsony lowers both employment and wages; unions push wages up.
  • Careful comparisons, controlling for productivity, are needed to measure discrimination.

Income Distribution

You will learn to

  • Define poverty and the poverty line
  • Explain the poverty trap and how the safety net is designed
  • Measure inequality with quintiles and the Lorenz curve
  • Explain causes of rising inequality and policies to reduce it

Markets reward productivity, but they don't guarantee that everyone has enough. Poverty and inequality are related but different problems.

Poverty

The U.S. poverty line, devised by Mollie Orshansky in 1963, is three times the cost of an adequate diet, adjusted for family size and inflation. In 2015 it was $11,790 for one person and $25,240 for a family of four. The poverty rate hasn't fallen below 11% in four decades; it peaked at 15.9% in 2011 and was 14.5% in 2013, with higher rates for children (19.9%), Black (27.1%) and Hispanic (23.5%) Americans. Worldwide, about half of people live on less than $2.50 a day. The official U.S. measure counts only cash income, so it leaves out food aid, Medicaid and housing help.

The poverty trap

If benefits fall by $1 for every $1 earned, working gains nothing: the poverty trap. A single mother earning $8 an hour with an $18,000 guarantee would have exactly $18,000 whether she works 0 or 2,000 hours. If benefits fall only 50 cents per dollar earned, every hour of work raises her income. The trade-off: slower phase-outs cost the government more.

Why inequality has grown

About half the rise in U.S. household inequality comes from changing families: more two-earner couples, high earners marrying each other, and more single-parent households. The rest reflects a widening skill gap. The number of bachelor's degrees awarded rose more than 90% between 1970 and 2014, yet college workers' pay premium still grew, because demand for skilled labor rose even faster, driven by information technology and globalization.

Policies

Governments reduce inequality through redistribution (a progressive tax system plus programs like the EITC), a "ladder of opportunity" (preschool, schools, college aid, retraining, anti-discrimination laws) and the estate tax (which in 2013 applied only to estates above $5.25 million). Heavy redistribution can weaken incentives to work, but some policies, such as good public education, can raise equality and output together. Wealth (assets minus debts) is even more unequal than income.

Key takeaways

  • Steep benefit phase-outs create a poverty trap; the EITC rewards work.
  • Quintile shares and the Lorenz curve measure inequality, which has risen in the U.S. since 1980.
  • Family changes and fast-growing demand for skills explain much of the rise.

Globalization and Trade

You will learn to

  • Distinguish absolute and comparative advantage
  • Calculate the gains from specialization and trade
  • Explain intra-industry trade
  • Analyze who wins and loses from trade, tariffs and quotas

Countries trade for the same reason people do: specializing in what you do relatively best leaves everyone with more.

Absolute vs comparative advantage

A country has an absolute advantage if it produces a good with fewer resources (Canada makes 100 lb of beef with 2 ranchers; Argentina needs 3). It has a comparative advantage if its opportunity cost is lower. David Ricardo showed in 1817 that trade benefits both partners even when one is better at everything.

Intra-industry trade

About 60% of U.S. and European trade is intra-industry trade: the U.S. both exports and imports cars, food and machinery. Similar economies gain by specializing in narrow product niches, by splitting up the value chain (an iPhone is designed in the U.S., uses Korean parts and is assembled in China), and through economies of scale, while consumers get more variety and competition.

Supply and demand in trade

Without trade, sugar costs 12¢ per pound in Brazil and 24¢ in the U.S. With trade, the price settles at 16¢ in both: Brazil exports 15 tons and the U.S. imports 15. Brazilian producers and U.S. consumers gain; Brazilian consumers and U.S. producers lose. Total surplus rises in both countries, but trade has income distribution effects.

Protectionism

Protectionism limits imports through tariffs (import taxes), import quotas (quantity limits, such as the U.S. sugar quota) and nontariff barriers (rules, inspections, paperwork). Justifications include saving jobs, infant industries and national security. Protection helps the protected producers but costs consumers and firms that use the goods as inputs, generally more than the producers gain. Despite heavy apparel protection, U.S. textile and apparel jobs fell 42% from 2007 to 2012.

Trade agreements

The World Trade Organization (1995, successor to GATT) negotiates lower barriers in rounds; average tariffs in industrialized countries fell from about 40% in 1946 to under 5% by 1990. Regional deals range from free trade agreements (NAFTA) to common markets and economic unions (the EU). Antidumping rules let countries impose tariffs on imports sold below cost.

Key takeaways

  • Comparative advantage, not absolute advantage, drives gains from trade.
  • Trade raises total surplus but creates winners and losers.
  • Tariffs and quotas raise domestic prices, protect producers and cost consumers more than producers gain.

Exchange Rates and International Finance

You will learn to

  • Explain who supplies and demands currencies
  • Identify who wins and loses when a currency strengthens or weakens
  • Predict exchange-rate changes from expectations, interest rates and inflation
  • Compare exchange-rate policies

An exchange rate is just a price: the price of one currency in terms of another.

The foreign exchange market

Every international transaction demands one currency and supplies another. The dollar is demanded by foreigners buying U.S. exports, visiting the U.S., or investing here, through foreign direct investment (owning 10% or more of a firm) or portfolio investment (purely financial). Americans buying imports, traveling or investing abroad supply dollars. About $5.3 trillion trades every day, mostly portfolio flows between banks, and the dollar is on one side of about 88% of trades. Firms reduce exchange-rate risk by hedging.

Strong and weak currencies

When a currency appreciates (strengthens), it buys more foreign currency; when it depreciates (weakens), less. If the dollar weakens from $1.50 to $2.00 per pound, a $25,000 Ford pickup falls from £16,667 to £12,500 in Britain, while a £6 six-pack of British ale rises from $9 to $12 in the U.S. A strong dollar helps U.S. importers and tourists abroad but hurts U.S. exporters. As the course puts it, a stronger currency isn't necessarily better, just different.

In the long run, arbitrage pushes exchange rates toward purchasing power parity (PPP), the rate at which traded goods cost the same everywhere. PPP rates also make GDP comparisons more reliable: Japan's ¥500 trillion GDP is $4.6 trillion at 108 yen per dollar but $5.5 trillion at 90 yen per dollar.

Why exchange rates matter

Exchange rates affect exports and therefore aggregate demand. Sudden swings disrupt business plans, and a currency collapse can break banks that borrowed in dollars but lent in their own currency, as in East Asia in 1997–98 and Argentina in 2002.

  • Exchange rates are set by supply and demand for currencies.
  • Appreciation helps importers and hurts exporters; depreciation does the reverse.
  • Higher interest rates and expected appreciation strengthen a currency; higher inflation weakens it.
