Module 1

Preferences and Indifference Curves

You will learn to

  • State the basic assumptions economists make about preferences
  • Draw and read indifference curves, and explain why they slope down, never cross and bow in
  • Calculate and interpret the marginal rate of substitution (MRS)
  • Recognize the special cases of perfect substitutes and perfect complements

Consumer theory begins with one question: faced with two bundles of goods, which one does a person prefer?

Goods, bundles and preferences

A good is anything a consumer likes to have more of. (A bad, like pollution, is the reverse; we can treat bads as the absence of a good.) A bundle is a particular combination of quantities of goods, such as 5 burritos and 4 sandwiches in a week. To keep pictures simple we work with two goods at a time. Everything else the person buys is lumped together as a second "good", so the model still covers real budgets.

Economics does not ask why someone likes what they like. It only asks that their rankings follow a few reasonable rules:

AssumptionMeaningWhy it matters
CompletenessFor any two bundles, the person can say A is preferred, B is preferred, or they are equally good (indifferent)Every pair can be compared
TransitivityIf A is preferred to B and B to C, then A is preferred to CRankings are consistent, so curves can't cross

Indifference curves

An indifference curve connects all bundles that give the same satisfaction. Suppose Luca is indifferent between 5 burritos and 4 sandwiches in a week, and 3 burritos and 5 sandwiches. Both bundles sit on the same curve. A bundle with more of both lies on a higher curve, and he would prefer it.

  • Downward sloping. Because more is better, if you take away some of one good you must give some of the other to keep the person equally happy.
  • Higher is better. Curves farther from the origin represent more satisfaction.
  • They never cross. If two curves crossed, the crossing bundle would be equal to bundles that are clearly better and worse than each other, which breaks transitivity.
  • Bowed toward the origin. A person who likes variety is willing to give up a lot of a good they have plenty of, and little of one they are short of.

The marginal rate of substitution

The marginal rate of substitution (MRS) is how many units of one good a person will give up for one more unit of the other, while staying equally well off. It is the (absolute) slope of the indifference curve.

MRS = ΔA ÷ ΔB (units of A given up per extra unit of B)Luca: from (5 burritos, 4 sandwiches) to (3 burritos, 5 sandwiches), he gives up 2 burritos for 1 sandwich, so MRS = 2.

Because curves bow in, the MRS falls as you move down and to the right along a curve: when Luca has lots of burritos he'll trade several for a sandwich, but when burritos are scarce he'll trade far fewer. This is diminishing marginal rate of substitution.

Worked example. Mia is indifferent among these bundles of coffee (C) and muffins (M): (2, 12), (3, 8), (4, 6), (6, 4). Moving from (2, 12) to (3, 8), she gives up 4 muffins for 1 coffee: MRS = 4. From (3, 8) to (4, 6), MRS = 2. From (4, 6) to (6, 4), she gives up 2 muffins for 2 coffees: MRS = 1. The MRS is falling, so her curve is bowed in.

Special preferences

Perfect substitutesThe goods are interchangeable at a constant rate (two brands of identical bottled water). Indifference curves are straight lines; the MRS never changes.
Perfect complementsThe goods are only useful together, in a fixed ratio (left and right shoes). Curves are L-shaped; extra units of one good alone add nothing.

Key takeaways

  • Preferences are assumed complete, transitive and "more is better"; variety makes curves bow in.
  • Indifference curves slope down, never cross, and higher curves are better.
  • The MRS is the slope of the curve: how much of one good you will trade for one more of the other. It falls as you move along a standard curve.
Module 2

Utility

You will learn to

  • Explain what a utility function is, and why only the ranking matters
  • Write down the three standard utility functions and draw their indifference curves
  • Compute marginal utility and explain diminishing marginal utility
  • Show that MRS = MUA ÷ MUB

A utility function is a compact way to describe preferences: it gives each bundle a number, and higher numbers are better.

Utility functions

A utility function U(A, B) assigns a number to every bundle of two goods A and B. Utility is ordinal: the number only tells us the ranking, not how much better one bundle is. If U(bundle 1) = 20 and U(bundle 2) = 10, bundle 1 is preferred, but it isn't "twice as good". So if you replace U with 3U + 10, or with U², you get exactly the same preferences, and the same indifference curves.

An indifference curve is simply a set of bundles with the same utility, so each curve is a "contour line" of the utility function, like a line on a topographic map.

Three standard utility functions

PreferencesUtility functionIndifference curvesMRS
Cobb-Douglas ("typical")U = AαBβSmooth, bowed in(α/β)(B/A), falls as A rises
Perfect substitutesU = αA + βBStraight linesα/β, constant

Cobb-Douglas is the workhorse: it captures "likes variety" with just two numbers, and the larger α is relative to β, the more the person values A.

Marginal utility

Marginal utility (MU) is the extra utility from one more unit of a good, holding the other fixed: MUA = ΔU ÷ ΔA. For most goods it shows diminishing marginal utility: the tenth slice of pizza adds less than the first. (Total utility still rises, only more slowly.)

Why MRS = MUA ÷ MUB

Slide a little way along an indifference curve: utility doesn't change. Gaining ΔA of good A adds MUA × ΔA to utility, and giving up ΔB of good B takes away MUB × ΔB. Because the two exactly offset, MUA × ΔA = MUB × ΔB. Rearranged, the amount of B you will give up for one more unit of A is ΔB ÷ ΔA = MUA ÷ MUB.

MRS = MUA ÷ MUBWith A on the horizontal axis and B on the vertical axis, this is the (absolute) slope of the indifference curve: the units of B traded for one more A. We use this convention throughout the book.

Math extension

With U = AαBβ, the marginal utilities are the partial derivatives: MUA = α Aα−1Bβ and MUB = β AαBβ−1. Dividing, MUA ÷ MUB = (α/β)(B/A). For U = √(AB), α = β = ½, so the MRS is simply B ÷ A.

Worked example. Isaac has U = √(A·B) with A = apples and B = bananas. At the bundle (4 apples, 16 bananas), U = √64 = 8 and MRS = B/A = 16/4 = 4: he would give up 4 bananas for 1 more apple. At (16, 4), still U = 8, but MRS = 4/16 = ¼: he would give up only a quarter of a banana. Same utility, very different willingness to trade, because he has few apples in the first bundle and many in the second.

Ordinal does not mean arbitrary

A few facts make utility functions useful without ever measuring "happiness". (1) Only rankings matter, so the choices a utility-maximizer makes don't depend on which version of the function you use. (2) The MRS does have a meaning: it is the rate at which the person will actually trade, which can be observed. (3) We can compare the same person's utilities across their own bundles, but not one person's utility with another's.

Key takeaways

  • A utility function ranks bundles; only the ordering matters, so utility is ordinal.
  • Marginal utility usually diminishes. For Cobb-Douglas U = AαBβ, MRS = (α/β)(B/A).
  • Indifference curves are contour lines of utility. Their slope equals the ratio of marginal utilities.
Module 3

Budget Constraints and Consumer Choice

You will learn to

  • Draw a budget line and read its intercepts and slope
  • Show how a change in income or in a price moves the budget line
  • Find the utility-maximizing bundle using tangency (MRS = price ratio)
  • Solve for demand with Cobb-Douglas preferences, and handle corner solutions

Preferences say what a consumer wants; the budget says what she can afford. The best choice combines the two.

The budget constraint

With income I, prices PA and PB, a consumer can afford any bundle with PA·A + PB·B ≤ I. The budget line is the set of bundles that use up all the income. Its two intercepts are what she could buy if she spent everything on one good, I ÷ PA and I ÷ PB.

PA·A + PB·B = I   ⇒   B = I/PB − (PA/PB)·AThe slope −PA ÷ PB is the opportunity cost of A in terms of B, also called the economic rate of substitution (ERS).
ChangeEffect on the budget line
Income risesParallel shift outward (same slope, bigger set of affordable bundles)
Price of A fallsPivots outward on the A-axis; B-intercept unchanged; line gets flatter
Price of B risesPivots inward on the B-axis; A-intercept unchanged

The consumer choice problem

The consumer picks the affordable bundle on the highest indifference curve. Two things are true at the best bundle:

  1. It lies on the budget line, because more is better (she spends everything).
  2. The indifference curve just touches the budget line, so the two have the same slope.
MRS = PA ÷ PB   ⇔   MUA ÷ PA = MUB ÷ PB"Equal bang for the buck": the last dollar spent on each good must yield the same extra utility.

Why? If MRS > PA/PB, the consumer values A more than the market charges for it, so she should swap some B for A, until the two rates match. Take a pizza and a salad that each cost $1. If the next pizza adds less utility than the next salad, shifting a dollar from pizza to salad makes her better off. She keeps shifting until the marginal utilities per dollar are equal.

Cobb-Douglas demand

For U = AαBβ, setting MRS = (α/β)(B/A) equal to PA/PB and plugging into the budget gives the demand functions:

A* = [α ÷ (α+β)] · I ÷ PA     B* = [β ÷ (α+β)] · I ÷ PBThe consumer spends a fixed share of income on each good: α/(α+β) on A and β/(α+β) on B.
Worked example. I = $120, PA = $3, PB = $4, and U = A0.5B0.5. She spends half her income on each: A* = ½ × 120 ÷ 3 = 20, B* = ½ × 120 ÷ 4 = 15. Check: MRS = B/A = 15/20 = 0.75 = PA/PB = 3/4. ✓

Corner solutions and kinks

  • Perfect substitutes (U = αA + βB): the MRS is constant, so tangency almost never holds. Compare the utility per dollar of each good: spend everything on whichever has the higher MU ÷ price. (Two equally liked sodas with Coke at $1 and Pepsi at $1.50: buy only Coke.) If the ratios tie, every bundle on the budget line is equally good.
  • Perfect complements (U = min[αA, βB]): she wastes nothing by buying in fixed proportions, so she chooses the kink where αA = βB. Combined with the budget line this gives A* = βI ÷ (βPA + αPB) and B* = αI ÷ (βPA + αPB). A rise in the price of either good reduces how much of both she buys.

Policy example: hybrid cars and the rebound effect

Suppose the government gives a tax credit for buying fuel-efficient hybrid cars, hoping to cut gasoline use and carbon emissions. Think of a consumer choosing between miles driven and everything else. A more efficient car lowers the cost of each mile, which pivots the budget line outward along the miles axis. By the logic of consumer choice, the consumer will typically drive more and buy more of other goods too. This is the rebound effect: some of the fuel savings are lost because driving became cheaper.

The size of the rebound is an empirical question. Studies summarized in the source text suggest a 10% improvement in miles per gallon raises driving by roughly 1–3%, so fuel use still falls, but by less than the engineering numbers promise. Economists therefore often prefer a gasoline tax, which raises the cost of driving, over efficiency subsidies, to cut fuel use at lower cost.

Key takeaways

  • The budget line has slope −PA/PB; income shifts it, and a price change pivots it.
  • The best bundle is on the budget line where MRS = PA/PB (equal MU per dollar).
  • Cobb-Douglas consumers spend constant shares of income; perfect substitutes give corner solutions; perfect complements give a kink solution.
Module 4

Individual and Market Demand

You will learn to

  • Derive an individual demand curve from the consumer choice problem
  • Separate a price change into a substitution effect and an income effect
  • Classify goods as normal, inferior or (rarely) Giffen
  • Add individual demands into market demand and measure elasticities

A demand curve is not an assumption. It is what comes out when we ask how the best bundle changes as a price changes.

From choices to a demand curve

Fix income and the price of B, and let the price of A fall one step at a time. Each time, the budget line pivots outward and the consumer picks a new best bundle. Plot the price of A against the quantity chosen and you have the person's demand curve for A. It slopes down for almost every good. A change in the good's own price moves us along the curve. A change in income, in the price of another good, or in tastes shifts the whole curve.

Income changes: normal and inferior goods

Tracing the best bundle as income rises gives the income consumption curve, and plotting the quantity of one good against income gives its Engel curve. If the quantity rises with income, the good is a normal good; if it falls, it is an inferior good (instant noodles, bus rides, used clothes, for those who can afford better). For Cobb-Douglas preferences every good is normal, because each good's spending is a fixed share of income.

Substitution and income effects

When the price of A rises, the consumer buys less A for two reasons.

  • The substitution effect: A is now relatively more expensive, so she swaps toward B, staying on the same indifference curve. This effect always points the "obvious" way: a higher price means less of the good.
  • The income effect: the price rise cuts her purchasing power, so she is effectively poorer and moves to a lower indifference curve. For a normal good, that means buying less of A too; for an inferior good, it means buying more.

To separate them graphically, draw a hypothetical budget line with the new slope that is just tangent to the old indifference curve. The move from the old bundle to that tangent point is the substitution effect; the move from there to the final bundle (on the actual new budget line) is the income effect.

Math extension

The Slutsky equation writes this decomposition as ∂A/∂PA = (∂A/∂PA)U fixed − A·(∂A/∂I): the total effect equals the substitution effect (the compensated, utility-constant response, always negative) minus the quantity bought times the income response. If A is inferior, ∂A/∂I < 0, the second term is positive and can, in principle, overpower the first.

Giffen goods

If a good is so inferior that the income effect outweighs the substitution effect, a higher price could raise the quantity demanded. Such a Giffen good is a theoretical curiosity: the classic example is a staple food that takes up most of a very poor household's budget. Clear real-world cases are extremely rare, and the law of demand holds nearly everywhere.

Market demand

Market demand is the sum of all consumers' individual demands. Graphically, add the curves horizontally: at each price, add up the quantities. It slopes down because each individual curve does, and it also grows with the number of consumers.

Key takeaways

  • A demand curve traces the best bundle as the price of one good changes; own-price changes are movements along it, everything else shifts it.
  • Every price change has a substitution effect (always toward the cheaper good) and an income effect (sign depends on whether the good is normal or inferior).
  • Market demand is the horizontal sum of individual demands; elasticities summarize how responsive demand is.
Module 5

Production and Technology

You will learn to

  • Describe a production function with labor and capital
  • Distinguish the short run from the long run
  • Compute total, marginal and average product and explain diminishing marginal product
  • Draw isoquants, find the MRTS, and classify returns to scale

Consumer theory told us where demand comes from. To understand supply, we need to know what firms can make from their inputs.

Inputs and production functions

A firm turns inputs into output. Economists group inputs into two broad kinds: labor (L), all human effort from the factory floor to the lawyer's desk, and capital (K), the machines, buildings and tools used to produce. A production function tells you the most output (Q) possible from any combination, written Q = f(L, K).

An input is fixed if the firm can't change it in the period in question (a factory under lease) and variable if it can (hours worked). In the short run at least one input is fixed, usually capital. In the long run every input can be changed. The two are not calendar periods: they depend on the firm and the input.

The short run: one variable input

Holding capital fixed, add workers one at a time. Three measures describe what happens:

  • Total product: all the output, Q.
  • Marginal product (MPL): the extra output from one more worker, ΔQ ÷ ΔL.
  • Average product (APL): output per worker, Q ÷ L.

The long run: two variable inputs

With both labor and capital variable, the firm can reach the same output in many ways. An isoquant ("equal quantity") shows all the combinations of L and K that produce a given Q. Isoquants share the properties of indifference curves: they slope down (you can swap one input for the other), they never cross, higher ones mean more output, and they bow in (the inputs are not perfectly interchangeable).

The slope is the marginal rate of technical substitution (MRTS): the amount of capital a firm can give up when it adds one worker, keeping output unchanged. It equals the ratio of marginal products, MRTS = MPL ÷ MPK. Just as for consumers, the isoquant is straight when inputs are perfect substitutes (a robot or a worker does the same task) and L-shaped when they are perfect complements (one driver per truck).

Returns to scale

Suppose you double all inputs. If output more than doubles, the technology has increasing returns to scale; if it exactly doubles, constant returns; if it less than doubles, decreasing returns. This is different from diminishing marginal product, which concerns raising one input while the others stay fixed. For the Cobb-Douglas function Q = LaKb, scaling every input by t multiplies output by ta+b. So returns are increasing if a + b > 1, constant if a + b = 1 and decreasing if a + b < 1.

Math extension

For Q = A·LaKb: MPL = a·Q/L and MPK = b·Q/K, so MRTS = (a/b)(K/L). For Q = 10L½K½, MRTS = K/L. Each marginal product falls as its own input rises (a, b < 1), yet returns to scale can still be constant when a + b = 1.

Key takeaways

  • In the short run capital is fixed, and marginal product eventually diminishes as more labor is added.
  • Isoquants show the input combinations that make the same output; their slope is the MRTS = MPL ÷ MPK.
  • Returns to scale compare output with the scale of all inputs: Cobb-Douglas has constant returns if a + b = 1.
Module 6

Minimizing Costs and Cost Curves

You will learn to

  • Draw isocost lines and find the cost-minimizing mix of labor and capital
  • Predict how the input mix responds to a change in input prices
  • Derive total, average and marginal cost curves, in the short run and the long run
  • Explain economies and diseconomies of scale

A firm that wants to maximize profit must first produce whatever output it chooses at the lowest possible cost.

Isocost lines

If labor costs w per hour and capital costs r per hour, the total cost of using L hours of labor and K hours of capital is C = wL + rK. An isocost line joins all the input combinations that cost the same. It is the producer's version of the budget line, with slope −w ÷ r. With w = $20 and r = $10, giving up 2 units of capital pays for 1 more unit of labor, so the slope is −2. Isocost lines farther from the origin cost more.

The cost-minimization problem

The firm needs a given output Q. It picks the point on that isoquant that lies on the lowest isocost line. At the best point the isoquant and isocost line are tangent:

MRTS = w ÷ r   ⇔   MPL ÷ w = MPK ÷ rThe last dollar spent on labor and on capital must add the same extra output. Otherwise the firm could cut cost by shifting spending.
Root beer. A brewer has Q = 10·L½K½, pays w = $20 and r = $5, and wants 1,000 barrels. MRTS = K/L, so tangency requires K/L = 20/5 = 4, i.e. K = 4L. Plug into the production function: 1,000 = 10·L½(4L)½ = 20L, so L = 50 and K = 200. Cost = 20(50) + 5(200) = $2,000.

When input prices or output change

If capital's rental rate rises from $5 to $10, tangency becomes K/L = 20/10 = 2, so K = 2L. The same 1,000 barrels now take L ≈ 70.7 and K ≈ 141.4, costing about $2,828 (rounding to 71 and 142 gives about $2,840). The firm substitutes toward labor, but cost still rises, since one input became more expensive. Raising output traces an expansion path: with K = 4L, output is Q = 20L, so L = Q/20 and K = Q/5. Doubling Q doubles both inputs, and the long-run total cost is C(Q) = 2Q. Average cost is a constant $2, which is what constant returns to scale means.

Math extension

Minimize wL + rK subject to Q = f(L, K). The Lagrangian gives MPL = λw and MPK = λr, so MPL/MPK = w/r. For Q = 10L½K½, the input demands are L* = (Q/10)(r/w)½ and K* = (Q/10)(w/r)½, and the cost function is C(Q) = (Q/5)(wr)½. A higher wage lowers labor and raises capital use, and a doubling of Q doubles each input.

The same logic as for consumers handles the corners: with perfect substitutes, use only the input that is cheaper per unit of output; with perfect complements, use inputs in the fixed ratio, and the tangency rule does not apply.

Short run versus long run

Each choice of K̄ gives its own short-run cost curve. If LRAC falls as output grows there are economies of scale; if it is flat, constant returns to scale; if it rises, diseconomies of scale.

Profit Maximization, Supply and Perfect Competition

You will learn to

  • Distinguish accounting and economic profit
  • Find the profit-maximizing output of a price-taking firm
  • Apply the shutdown rule and derive the firm's and the industry's supply curves
  • Explain entry and exit, long-run zero profit, and the efficiency of perfect competition

A competitive firm has one decision: how much to produce. The answer gives us the supply curve.

Profit

Profit is total revenue minus total cost: π = TR − TC. Economists count all costs, including the opportunity cost of the owner's own time and money (implicit costs). Profit calculated this way is economic profit; the accountant's version, which subtracts only out-of-pocket (explicit) costs, is accounting profit.

The soap business. A craft soap maker has $230,000 in sales and $155,000 in out-of-pocket costs: accounting profit = $75,000. But the owner gave up a $90,000 job, so total economic cost = $155,000 + $90,000 = $245,000 and economic profit = −$15,000. Starting the business was a mistake, even though the books show a profit.

The competitive firm

A market is perfectly competitive when there are many buyers and sellers, the product is identical across sellers, firms can enter and leave freely, and everyone has full information and negligible transaction costs. Then each firm is a price taker: no single firm is large enough to move the market price, so its revenue from each extra unit is simply the price. Marginal revenue equals price, MR = P.

The profit-maximizing rule

Compare the extra revenue and the extra cost of one more unit. If a car sells for MR = $17,000 and its marginal cost is $15,500, building it adds $1,500 to profit. If marginal cost is $18,000, it subtracts $1,000. Profit is greatest where MR = MC, on the rising part of the MC curve. For a price taker this is:

P = MC(q*)Profit = (P − ATC) × q*: positive when price exceeds average total cost, zero when equal, negative when below.

The shutdown rule

In the short run, the fixed cost has to be paid whether or not the firm produces. If it shuts down it loses exactly FC. If it keeps producing, the loss is FC minus whatever revenue exceeds variable cost. So the firm should keep producing as long as TR ≥ VC, i.e. P ≥ AVC. A shop with a $1,000 lease that earns enough to cover all its variable costs and $500 of the lease loses $500 a month by operating, versus $1,000 by closing, so it should stay open. The firm's short-run supply curve is the part of its MC curve above minimum AVC; below that, it supplies zero. Industry supply in the short run is the horizontal sum of the firms' supplies.

Long-run supply and market equilibrium

In the long run, all costs are variable and firms can enter and exit. When price is above ATC, economic profits attract entry; market supply shifts right and price falls. When price is below ATC, losses cause exit; supply shifts left and price rises. The process stops when P = minimum ATC: economic profit is zero, which means every owner earns exactly what their resources could earn elsewhere. Two things are in equilibrium at once: the market price clears the market, and the number of firms stops changing.

Constant, increasing and decreasing cost industries

If all firms are identical and input prices don't change, the long-run industry supply curve is horizontal at the zero-profit price. More often, an expanding industry bids up input prices (an increasing-cost industry) or the firms differ, and long-run supply slopes upward. If a bigger industry lowers input costs (a decreasing-cost industry), long-run supply can slope down.

Efficiency

In long-run competitive equilibrium, firms produce at minimum average cost (productive efficiency), and price equals marginal cost (allocative efficiency): the value of the last unit to consumers equals its cost of production. Module 8 shows this is also the point that maximizes total surplus.

Key takeaways

  • Economic profit subtracts implicit costs too. A price taker maximizes profit where P = MR = MC.
  • Shut down in the short run only if P < AVC; supply is MC above minimum AVC.
  • Entry and exit drive long-run price to minimum ATC and economic profit to zero.

Market Equilibrium and Welfare

You will learn to

  • Find the equilibrium price and quantity from demand and supply equations
  • Predict the effect of shifts in demand, supply, or both
  • Measure consumer, producer and total surplus, and define Pareto efficiency
  • Analyze price ceilings, price floors and taxes, including deadweight loss

Demand comes from consumers (Modules 1–4) and supply from firms (Modules 5–7). Put them together and we get prices, quantities and welfare.

Market equilibrium

A market brings together buyers and sellers of a good. Equilibrium is the price at which the quantity demanded equals the quantity supplied. Take demand QD = 3,000 − 50P and supply QS = 50P − 1,000. Setting them equal: 3,000 − 50P = 50P − 1,000, so 100P = 4,000 and P* = $40. Plugging in, QS = 50(40) − 1,000 = 1,000 and QD = 3,000 − 2,000 = 1,000, so Q* = 1,000. Above $40 there is a surplus and sellers cut prices; below $40 there is a shortage and buyers bid prices up.

Comparative statics

Comparative statics compares equilibria before and after a change. A demand shift can come from tastes, income (normal vs inferior goods), the price of substitutes or complements, or the number of buyers. A supply shift comes from input prices, technology, regulation or the number of firms. When only one curve shifts, the result is clear; when both shift, one of price or quantity is ambiguous.

Welfare: consumer, producer and total surplus

The height of the demand curve is what buyers are willing to pay for each unit; the height of the supply curve is what sellers must receive (their marginal cost). Consumer surplus is the area below demand and above price. Producer surplus is the area above supply and below price. Their sum is total surplus. In our market: consumer surplus = ½ × 1,000 × ($60 − $40) = $10,000 and producer surplus = ½ × 1,000 × ($40 − $20) = $10,000, so total surplus is $20,000.

An allocation is Pareto efficient if nobody can be made better off without making someone else worse off. At the competitive equilibrium every trade that benefits both a buyer and a seller has been made, and no further gains are possible, so total surplus is as large as it can be. Any other quantity leaves some trades unmade: the lost surplus is the deadweight loss (DWL).

Price ceilings and floors

A price ceiling is a legal maximum price; it matters only if it is set below equilibrium, where it creates a shortage (excess demand). A rent ceiling means fewer apartments are rented than at the market price, some lucky renters gain, others are shut out, and there is deadweight loss. A price floor is a legal minimum; set above equilibrium it creates a surplus (excess supply), as with a minimum wage (Module 9) or a farm price support.

Taxes

A per-unit tax drives a wedge between the price buyers pay and the price sellers keep. It doesn't matter whether the tax is collected from buyers or sellers: the tax incidence is the same. The burden falls more heavily on the side of the market that is less elastic, the side with fewer alternatives. A tax shrinks the quantity traded, raises revenue t × Q, and creates deadweight loss on the units that no longer trade. In our market, a $20 tax cuts the quantity from 1,000 to 500, buyers pay $50 and sellers keep $30 (the burden is split evenly because the curves are equally steep), revenue is $10,000, and DWL = ½ × $20 × 500 = $5,000.

Key takeaways

  • Equilibrium is where QD = QS; shifts in one or both curves can be analyzed with comparative statics.
  • The competitive equilibrium maximizes total surplus (consumer + producer): it is Pareto efficient.
  • Binding ceilings and floors, and taxes, reduce the quantity traded and create deadweight loss; the less elastic side bears more of a tax.

Input Markets

You will learn to

  • Model a worker's labor-leisure choice and explain income and substitution effects of a wage change
  • Derive a firm's labor demand from marginal revenue product
  • Analyze a competitive labor market and the minimum wage
  • Explain monopsony and why minimum wages can raise employment there

Households supply labor and firms demand it. The "price" of labor is the wage, and the same tools of supply and demand apply.

Labor supply: the labor-leisure choice

Suppose a worker has 24 hours a day to divide between leisure (l) and work (H = 24 − l). An hour of work earns the wage w, so each hour of leisure costs w in forgone income. Her budget line has slope −w. Preferences over income (I) and leisure are drawn like those over any two goods, and she picks the best bundle on the line: the same consumer problem as in Module 3, with income itself now a choice.

When the wage rises, two effects pull in opposite directions:

  • Substitution effect: leisure is now more expensive, so she works more.
  • Income effect: she is richer for any given hours, and leisure is a normal good, so she takes more leisure and works less.

If the substitution effect wins, labor supply slopes up. If the income effect wins at high wages, the supply curve can bend backward. In practice, many workers cannot choose their hours freely, and estimates for some groups (such as prime-age men) put labor supply as nearly vertical.

Labor demand

Firms demand labor because they want what labor produces: labor demand is a derived demand. Hiring one more worker adds MPL units of output, which the firm sells at the marginal revenue MR. The extra revenue is the marginal revenue product of labor:

MRPL = MR × MPL   (for a price taker, MRPL = P × MPL)The firm hires until MRPL = w. For a competitive firm this is the same as P = w ÷ MPL = MC.

Because marginal product diminishes, MRPL slopes down, and so does labor demand. A change in the wage moves the firm along its demand curve; a change in the output price or in productivity shifts it. A competitive labor market is in equilibrium where market labor supply equals market labor demand.

The minimum wage

A minimum wage is a price floor in the labor market. If it is set above the competitive wage, firms demand fewer hours than workers want to supply: there is unemployment (the gap between the quantity of labor supplied and demanded), employment falls from the competitive level, and total surplus falls (a deadweight loss). Workers who keep their jobs gain; those who lose them do not. The more inelastic supply and demand are, the smaller the job losses, and many empirical studies find small effects. Because many minimum-wage workers live in higher-income households, economists often point to alternatives, like the earned income tax credit, that target poor households more directly. In 2015, Washington State's $9.47 was the highest state minimum, against the federal minimum of $7.25.

Monopsony

A monopsony is a market with a single buyer, here a single employer. To hire more workers it must pay a higher wage, to everyone. If a firm hires 1,000 hours at $20 (wage bill $20,000) and 1,200 hours at $25 (wage bill $30,000), the extra 200 hours cost $10,000, or $50 an hour: the marginal expenditure on labor ($50) is far above the wage ($25). The monopsonist hires where MRPL = marginal expenditure, then pays the lowest wage workers will accept for that many hours. The result is fewer hours and a lower wage than under competition.

This reverses the minimum-wage story. A minimum wage set between the monopsony wage and the competitive wage makes the marginal expenditure flat at the minimum wage, so the firm hires more workers at a higher wage. Only a minimum wage above the competitive level reduces employment. Unions, as sellers of labor with market power, can also push wages up in the same way.

Policy example: banning self-service gas

For decades Oregon (like New Jersey) banned self-service gasoline, while neighboring Washington allowed it. The ban raises the demand for gas station labor, but it also raises the cost of supplying gas, which shifts supply left. Professor Emerson compared Oregon with Washington (the comparison is approximate, since the states differ in other ways) and estimated that the ban added roughly 3 to 5 cents per gallon to what Oregonians paid. An analysis of the same data suggested Oregon had around 2,400 more gas station workers than it would if it matched Washington's per-capita figure (about 9,800 against 7,400). The jobs are visible; the small extra cost on every gallon is spread across all drivers. A later study of Oregon's 2018 rural repeal found prices fell by about 4.4 cents per gallon. Oregon has since allowed self-service statewide (2023). A good example of how an input-market rule shows up in the product market.

Key takeaways

  • A wage rise has a substitution effect (work more) and an income effect (work less); which wins determines the slope of labor supply.
  • Firms hire until the wage equals the marginal revenue product of labor.
  • A minimum wage above the competitive wage causes unemployment; under monopsony, a moderate minimum wage can raise both wages and employment.

General Equilibrium

You will learn to

  • Explain why markets are linked and why partial equilibrium can mislead
  • Use an Edgeworth box to find gains from trade, the contract curve and a competitive equilibrium
  • State the two welfare theorems
  • Show how comparative advantage makes trade beneficial using production possibility frontiers

So far we studied one market at a time. In reality a change in the market for gasoline changes the markets for cars, bus rides, and wages. General equilibrium looks at all markets at once.

Partial and general equilibrium

Partial equilibrium analysis studies one market and holds everything else fixed. It works well when the market is small. General equilibrium analysis finds prices in all markets at once, so that every consumer is maximizing utility, every firm is maximizing profit, and every market clears. A tax on one good, for example, shifts demand for its substitutes and complements, which shifts their prices, which feeds back to the original market.

Trade between two people: the Edgeworth box

Gilligan and Mary Ann are stranded on an island with 150 bananas and 100 coconuts. Gilligan starts out with 60 bananas and 60 coconuts; Mary Ann has 90 bananas and 40 coconuts. Both have the same preferences, U = ln(bananas) + 2·ln(coconuts), so each gets more utility from coconuts than from bananas. An Edgeworth box has the same dimensions as the total endowment, 150 wide and 100 tall. A point in the box is a split of the goods: Gilligan's bundle is measured from the bottom-left corner and Mary Ann's from the top-right corner, so every point gives each person exactly what the other doesn't have.

An allocation is Pareto efficient when no one can be made better off without making someone worse off. In the box, that happens where the two indifference curves are tangent, because there both people have the same marginal rate of substitution and no trade can make both happier. The set of all tangency points is the contract curve. With these preferences, MRSG = c/(2b) (coconuts per banana, Module 2); equating it across the two people gives the contract curve c = (2/3)·b for Gilligan's bundle.

The starting point (60, 60) is not on the contract curve, since Gilligan's MRS = 60/120 = 0.5 while Mary Ann's = 40/180 ≈ 0.22. Gilligan values bananas more highly relative to coconuts than Mary Ann does, so trades which give him bananas for coconuts make both better off. These are the gains from trade.

A competitive equilibrium

Suppose there is a market. Let coconuts be the numeraire (price 1) and let p be the price of a banana in coconuts. Each person sells their endowment at those prices and buys the best bundle with the proceeds, so wealth is WG = 60p + 60 for Gilligan and WM = 90p + 40 for Mary Ann. With U = ln b + 2 ln c, each spends 1/3 of wealth on bananas and 2/3 on coconuts: b = W/(3p), c = 2W/3.

For the market to clear, the total demand for bananas must equal 150. Gilligan and Mary Ann together demand (WG + WM)/(3p) = (150p + 100)/(3p) bananas. Setting this equal to 150 gives 150p + 100 = 450p, so p* = 1/3 coconut per banana (three bananas for one coconut). At that price Gilligan ends up with 80 bananas and 53⅓ coconuts and Mary Ann with 70 bananas and 46⅔ coconuts. Check: 80 + 70 = 150, 53⅓ + 46⅔ = 100, and both points sit on the contract curve (53⅓ = ⅔ × 80). Gilligan buys 20 bananas and gives up 6⅔ coconuts: a trade both want.

The two welfare theorems

  • First welfare theorem. Under standard conditions (price-taking, no externalities, complete markets, full information), any competitive equilibrium is Pareto efficient. Prices alone coordinate everyone's choices to a point on the contract curve, without anyone planning it.
  • Second welfare theorem. Under further conditions (such as convex preferences and technology), any Pareto efficient allocation can be reached as a competitive equilibrium if resources are first redistributed suitably. Efficiency and fairness can be treated separately: choose the fair split, then let markets do the rest.

Efficiency isn't the same as equality. A distribution where one person has everything is Pareto efficient too. Modules 11–16 look at the situations in which the first theorem's conditions fail.

Production and comparative advantage

Now let Gilligan and Mary Ann produce instead of find goods. A production possibilities frontier (PPF) shows the combinations of two goods an economy can make with its resources. Gilligan can make 200 bananas or 100 coconuts in a day (or a mix along a straight line); Mary Ann can make 100 bananas or 200 coconuts.

Gilligan gives up ½ coconut for each banana; Mary Ann gives up 2 coconuts for each banana. The one with the lower opportunity cost has the comparative advantage: Gilligan in bananas, Mary Ann in coconuts. Without trade each spends half the time on each good, and the island produces 100 + 50 = 150 bananas and 50 + 100 = 150 coconuts. With specialization, Gilligan makes 200 bananas and Mary Ann 200 coconuts. Total output rises to 200 of each, and as long as they trade at a price between the two opportunity costs, both gain. The same logic applies to countries: one doesn't need to be better at everything, only relatively better at something.

  • First welfare theorem: competitive equilibrium is efficient. Second: any efficient outcome can be reached through markets after redistribution.

Monopoly and Pricing Strategies

You will learn to

  • Explain where monopoly power comes from
  • Find a monopolist's profit-maximizing price and quantity, and the deadweight loss
  • Use the Lerner index and the inverse-elasticity rule
  • Analyze price discrimination, especially between two markets

A competitive firm takes the price as given. A monopolist is the only seller, so it chooses the price and has to accept the quantity demanded at that price.

Sources of monopoly power

  • Control of a key resource (a single water source, a mineral deposit).
  • Legal protection: patents (typically 20 years), copyrights and licenses give a temporary monopoly to reward innovation.
  • Network effects: a service is more valuable when more people use it.
  • Natural monopoly: fixed costs are so large relative to demand that one firm can serve the whole market at lower average cost than two could. Suppose a concrete-pumping business has an average cost of $6 per unit when one firm serves the market, but $11 when two firms split it. Only one firm can survive, and breaking it up would raise costs. Utilities such as water and electricity distribution are classic examples, and are usually regulated.

The monopolist's problem

The monopolist faces the whole market demand curve. To sell one more unit it has to lower the price on all units. So marginal revenue is less than the price: the extra unit brings in P, but lowers the revenue on all the earlier units. For a linear demand curve P = A − BQ, revenue is PQ = AQ − BQ², and MR = A − 2BQ, which has twice the slope of demand.

The profit-maximizing rule is still MR = MC. The monopolist finds the quantity where MR = MC and then reads the price off the demand curve. The price lies above marginal cost, which is the source of its profit and also the source of inefficiency.

Measuring market power

Rearranging MR = MC with the elasticity of demand ε (Module 4) gives the inverse-elasticity rule: MR = P(1 + 1/ε). The Lerner index measures the markup as a share of the price:

L = (P − MC) ÷ P = −1 ÷ εThe more elastic the demand (the easier it is for customers to leave), the smaller the markup. If ε = −4, then L = 25%; if MC = $30, the price is $30 ÷ 0.75 = $40.

Price discrimination

If the seller can charge different customers different prices for the same good, it can earn more. The firm needs market power, the ability to identify or sort customers, and a way of preventing resale. There are three standard types:

  • First-degree (perfect): each customer is charged exactly their willingness to pay. The seller captures all the surplus, and output is efficient.
  • Second-degree: the price depends on the quantity or version bought (bulk discounts, economy vs. first class). Customers sort themselves.
  • Third-degree: groups with different demands are charged different prices (student, senior or regional pricing).

For third-degree discrimination the monopolist applies MR = MC separately in each market. Because MR = P(1 + 1/ε), it ends up charging the higher price in the group with the less elastic demand.

Regulating monopoly

Governments respond in several ways: antitrust enforcement (blocking mergers, breaking up firms), price regulation of natural monopolies (setting the price at average cost, so the firm breaks even), public ownership, or simply letting patents expire. Setting the price at marginal cost would be efficient, but a natural monopoly with high fixed costs would lose money at that price, so regulators often settle for average-cost pricing or a subsidy.

Key takeaways

  • A monopolist's MR lies below demand; it produces where MR = MC and charges the price on the demand curve, so P > MC and there is deadweight loss.
  • The Lerner index (P − MC)/P equals −1/ε: less elastic demand means a bigger markup.
  • Price discrimination raises profit; in third-degree discrimination the less elastic group pays more.

Game Theory

You will learn to

  • Describe a game by its players, strategies and payoffs
  • Find dominant strategies and Nash equilibria in a payoff matrix
  • Explain the prisoner's dilemma and mixed strategies
  • Solve sequential games by backward induction and see when cooperation survives in repeated games

When a few players each affect one another's payoffs, a good choice depends on what the others do. Game theory is the tool for that.

The ingredients

A game has players, the strategies each can choose, and the payoffs (profit, utility) that result from every combination of choices. In a simultaneous-move game players choose without seeing the others' moves; in a sequential game they move in turn. A payoff matrix shows the first game: rows are one player's strategies, columns the other's, and each cell gives (row payoff, column payoff).

Dominant strategies and Nash equilibrium

A dominant strategy is best for a player whatever the other does. If every player has one, the outcome is easy to predict. Often nobody does, so we use a weaker idea: a Nash equilibrium is a set of strategies in which each player's choice is the best response to the others' choices, so that nobody wants to change alone. To find one, underline each player's best payoff in response to each of the other's strategies; a cell with both payoffs underlined is a Nash equilibrium. A game can have none, one, or several pure-strategy equilibria.

The prisoner's dilemma

Two firms can keep prices high (cooperate) or cut them (defect). Whatever the rival does, cutting is better for you, so cutting is a dominant strategy, and (Defect, Defect) is the Nash equilibrium. But both would be better off at (Cooperate, Cooperate). Individually rational choices lead to a collectively worse result. This is why cartels are unstable and why arms races, overfishing and ad wars are hard to stop.

Mixed strategies

In matching pennies, or a penalty kick, any predictable choice gets exploited. There is no Nash equilibrium in pure strategies, but there is one in mixed strategies, in which each player randomizes with probabilities that leave the other indifferent. Here it is 50–50.

Sequential games and credible threats

When one player moves first and the other can see it, we solve the game by backward induction: begin with the last mover's best choice, then work out what the earlier mover should do, knowing that. The result is a subgame-perfect equilibrium. It rules out non-credible threats, threats the player would not want to carry out when the time comes.

The entry game. A new firm decides whether to enter a market with an incumbent. If it stays out, it earns 0 and the incumbent earns 400. If it enters, the incumbent can accommodate (entrant 300, incumbent 200) or fight a price war (entrant −50, incumbent −100). In the matrix there are two Nash equilibria: (Enter, Accommodate) and (Stay out, Fight). But (Stay out, Fight) rests on a threat: once the entrant has entered, fighting gives the incumbent −100, which is worse than 200 for accommodating. Backward induction gives the single sensible outcome, (Enter, Accommodate), with payoffs 300 and 200.

Repeated games and cooperation

If the prisoner's dilemma is played once, defection wins. If the same firms meet again and again, reputation can sustain cooperation. A trigger strategy says: cooperate until the other side cheats, then punish by defecting forever. Suppose that cooperating pays C each period, cheating once pays T (the temptation), and after that the punishment payoff is P each period, with T > C > P. A player values future payoffs with a discount factor δ between 0 and 1 (the weight on next period relative to this one; patient players have δ near 1).

Cooperating forever is worth C ÷ (1 − δ). Cheating gives T now and P for ever after: T + δP ÷ (1 − δ). Cooperation is worth it when

δ ≥ δ* = (T − C) ÷ (T − P)With C = 100, T = 110 and P = 95: δ* = 10 ÷ 15 = 2/3. Firms that value the future at least this much will hold prices up.

The more patient the players, the bigger the punishment, and the smaller the temptation, the easier it is to sustain cooperation. This explains why cartels are easier to hold together in stable markets with few firms, and fall apart when firms are impatient (near bankruptcy) or cheating is hard to detect.

  • A Nash equilibrium is a set of strategies that are mutual best responses; a dominant strategy is best whatever the rival does.
  • In the prisoner's dilemma, self-interest produces an outcome worse for everyone; repetition can restore cooperation if δ ≥ (T − C)/(T − P).

Oligopoly and Monopolistic Competition

You will learn to

  • Describe the market structures between monopoly and perfect competition
  • Solve the Cournot (quantity), Stackelberg (leader–follower) and collusion outcomes
  • Explain the Bertrand price-competition result and why the number of firms matters
  • Show how entry drives monopolistic competition to zero profit

Most real markets are neither monopolies nor perfectly competitive. A few firms (oligopoly) or many firms selling slightly different products (monopolistic competition) are the norm, and the firms watch each other.

Oligopoly: competing on quantity

In an oligopoly, a handful of firms share the market, so each firm's choice moves the market price and affects its rivals' profits: this is a game (Module 12). In the Cournot model, firms choose quantities at the same time. Each firm treats its rivals' output as fixed and picks its own best quantity: its best-response function. A Nash equilibrium is where all best responses cross.

Let market demand be P = 1,000 − 2Q, with Q = q₁ + q₂, and marginal cost c = $400 for both firms. Firm 1's residual demand is P = (1,000 − 2q₂) − 2q₁, so its MR = (1,000 − 2q₂) − 4q₁. Setting MR = MC gives the best response q₁ = (600 − 2q₂) ÷ 4 = 150 − q₂/2. By symmetry q₂ = 150 − q₁/2. The equilibrium is q₁ = q₂ = 100, so Q = 200, P = $600 and each firm earns (600 − 400) × 100 = $20,000.

How many firms?

With n identical Cournot firms, each produces q = (A − c) ÷ ((n + 1)B), where demand is P = A − BQ. One firm gives the monopoly output; as n grows, the market output approaches the competitive output and the price approaches marginal cost. Market concentration is often summarized by the Herfindahl-Hirschman Index (HHI): the sum of the squared market shares (in percent). One firm gives 10,000; n equal firms give 10,000 ÷ n. Competition authorities use it to screen mergers: a merger which raises HHI sharply in an already concentrated market gets close scrutiny. Under Cournot the Lerner index equals the HHI share divided by the elasticity: concentration and markups move together.

Competing on price: Bertrand

If instead firms with identical products and the same constant marginal cost set prices, the answer changes sharply. Any firm that charges more than its rival loses all its customers, and a firm that undercuts slightly takes the whole market. The undercutting stops only at P = MC. So two firms are enough to get the competitive price: the Bertrand paradox. It disappears if products are differentiated, if firms face capacity limits, or if they meet repeatedly (Module 12), which is why real-world outcomes depend on how firms actually compete.

Monopolistic competition

In monopolistic competition, many firms sell differentiated products (restaurants, hair salons, brands of coffee) and entry is free. Each firm faces a downward-sloping demand curve for its own variety, so it prices above marginal cost like a monopolist. But entry erodes its demand: each new rival takes some customers away, and the firm's demand curve shifts in. Entry continues until profit is zero (P = ATC), though price still exceeds marginal cost and the firm produces below its minimum-ATC scale. Consumers pay a markup but get variety.

  • Cournot outcomes lie between competition and monopoly; more firms push the price toward marginal cost, and a leader (Stackelberg) does better than a follower.
  • Collusion earns the most but is unstable because each firm gains by cheating.

Externalities and Public Goods

You will learn to

  • Define positive and negative externalities and explain why markets over- or under-produce
  • Calculate the efficient quantity and deadweight loss, and design a Pigouvian tax or subsidy
  • Explain the Coase theorem and its limits
  • Explain the free-rider problem in public goods and common-resource overuse
  • Compare uniform rules, taxes and cap-and-trade for pollution

Competitive markets are efficient only if prices reflect all the costs and benefits. When they do not, the market gets it wrong. These are the situations that the First Welfare Theorem leaves out.

Externalities

An externality is a cost or benefit that a transaction imposes on someone who isn't part of it. A factory's smoke is a negative externality; a neighbor's well-kept garden or a vaccination is a positive externality. Because the decision-maker ignores the external effect, the private market quantity differs from the socially efficient one.

  • Negative externality in production: social marginal cost = private supply + external cost e. The market produces too much. The efficient quantity is where demand = social marginal cost.
  • Positive externality in consumption: social marginal benefit = private demand + external benefit e. The market produces too little.

Take the market from Module 8 (P = 60 − Q/50 and P = 20 + Q/50, market equilibrium Q = 1,000) and add an external cost of e per unit. Social marginal cost is 20 + Q/50 + e, so the efficient quantity solves 60 − Q/50 = 20 + Q/50 + e, giving Q = 1,000 − 25e. The deadweight loss of the market outcome is a triangle with base e and height 25e: DWL = ½ × e × 25e = 12.5e². For e = $10 it is $1,250. The loss grows with the square of the size of the externality, so small externalities are often not worth fixing.

Fixing externalities

  • Pigouvian tax or subsidy. A tax equal to the marginal external damage (a subsidy equal to the external benefit) makes private choices match social ones. In the lab, a tax of e on the polluter restores the efficient quantity.
  • Quantity regulation (standards, quotas) can reach the efficient quantity too, but only if the regulator knows enough to set it correctly.
  • Property rights and bargaining: the Coase theorem. If property rights are clearly assigned and bargaining is costless, the parties will negotiate to the efficient outcome whoever holds the rights; the assignment affects who pays, not what is produced. In practice, transaction costs and many affected parties (an entire city's air) limit this.

Public goods and common resources

Goods differ in two ways: whether people can be excluded from using them, and whether one person's use reduces what is left for others (rivalry).

Pollution control: standards, taxes and cap-and-trade

Reducing pollution should be done where it is cheapest. Take two firms that must together cut pollution by T units. Firm 1's total abatement cost is a₁² and firm 2's is 3a₂², so firm 2's cost is higher (a₁ + a₂ = T). A uniform standard makes each cut T/2. The least-cost split sets marginal abatement costs equal (2a₁ = 6a₂), which gives a₁ = 3T/4 and a₂ = T/4. For T = 60, the uniform rule costs 30² + 3 × 30² = $3,600 while the efficient split costs 45² + 3 × 15² = $2,700.

An emissions tax or cap-and-trade system reaches the efficient split without the regulator knowing each firm's costs: with tradable permits, firm 2 (with higher abatement costs) buys permits from firm 1, which cuts further. Trading stops where both firms face the same marginal cost, equal to the permit price.

Key takeaways

  • Negative externalities lead to overproduction and positive externalities to underproduction; the deadweight loss rises with the square of the size of the externality.
  • A Pigouvian tax equal to the marginal damage, clear property rights with low bargaining costs (Coase), or tradable permits can restore efficiency.
  • Public goods are under-provided because of free-riding; efficient provision sets the vertical sum of marginal benefits equal to marginal cost. Common resources are overused.

Uncertainty and Risk

You will learn to

  • Compute expected value, expected utility, the certainty equivalent and the risk premium
  • Relate risk aversion to the curvature of the utility function
  • Explain why risk-averse people buy insurance and why insurers can offer it
  • Find how much insurance a person should buy at a given premium

Many choices have uncertain outcomes: buying insurance, investing, picking a career. We need a way to compare gambles.

Expected value

The expected value (EV) of a gamble is the average of its payoffs, each weighted by its probability. A bag holds 10 marbles: 5 pay $0, 3 pay $50 and 2 pay $75. Then EV = 0.5 × 0 + 0.3 × 50 + 0.2 × 75 = $30. A 1% chance of losing $5,000 has an expected loss of 0.01 × 5,000 = $50. A gamble is actuarially fair if its price equals its expected value. If you played many times, you would break even on average.

But most people won't pay $30 to play the marble game if the payoffs were scaled up a thousand times. The value of the money to you is not the same as its dollar amount.

Expected utility and risk attitudes

Economists model the choice with expected utility: value each outcome by the utility u(x) of the money, and weight by probability. A person picks the gamble with the highest expected utility.

  • Risk averse: u is concave (diminishing marginal utility of money). A sure thing is preferred to a gamble with the same EV.
  • Risk neutral: u is linear. Only the EV matters.
  • Risk loving: u is convex. The gamble is preferred to its EV.

The certainty equivalent (CE) is the sure amount that gives the same utility as the gamble. The risk premium is EV − CE: the amount a risk-averse person would give up to avoid the risk.

A gamble. A 60% chance of $1,000 and a 40% chance of $2,500: EV = 0.6(1,000) + 0.4(2,500) = $1,600. With u = √x, expected utility = 0.6√1,000 + 0.4√2,500 = 18.97 + 20 = 38.97, while the sure $1,600 gives √1,600 = 40. The person prefers the sure $1,600. The certainty equivalent solves √CE = 38.97, so CE = 38.97² ≈ $1,519 and the risk premium is about $81.

Insurance

Insurance turns a risky outcome into a sure one. A risk-averse person with wealth W faces a loss L with probability p. Without insurance she has W with probability 1 − p and W − L with probability p. By paying a premium she can get a sure amount. She will be willing to pay more than the expected loss pL, up to the risk premium, which is why insurers can be profitable. A fair premium equals the expected payout; real premiums include a loading to cover administrative costs and profit.

With a fair premium, a risk-averse person buys full insurance, and her wealth is the same in both states. With a loaded premium she buys less than full coverage, because each extra dollar of coverage now costs more than its expected payout.

Why insurance markets can fail

Insurers need losses to be roughly independent so that the law of large numbers works. A flood hits everyone in the area at once, so private flood insurance is hard to provide and the US government runs the National Flood Insurance Program. Insurance can also fail when the buyer knows more than the seller: Module 16.

Key takeaways

  • Expected value averages payoffs; expected utility averages the utility of payoffs. Concave utility means risk aversion.
  • Certainty equivalent = the sure amount equal in utility to the gamble; the risk premium = EV − CE.
  • Risk-averse people pay more than the expected loss to insure; with a fair premium they fully insure, with a loaded premium they buy less.

Asymmetric Information and Time

You will learn to

  • Explain adverse selection and why the "market for lemons" can unravel
  • Distinguish adverse selection from moral hazard, and describe principal–agent problems
  • Describe signaling and screening as remedies
  • Compute present values and net present value, and explain the capital market

The efficiency results so far assumed everyone knows everything. They fail when one side knows more than the other, and when decisions stretch over time.

Asymmetric information

Information is asymmetric when one party in a transaction knows something relevant that the other doesn't. The seller of a used car knows its quality; the buyer doesn't. The buyer of health insurance knows her own health better than the insurer. Two problems follow:

  • Adverse selection: before the deal, hidden characteristics lead the wrong mix of people to take part. Those with bad risks or low-quality goods are the most eager to trade.
  • Moral hazard: after the deal, hidden actions change behavior. An insured driver takes less care; a manager spends the owner's money less carefully.

The market for lemons

Suppose half of used cars are good and half are "lemons". Good cars are worth $10,000 to a buyer and $8,000 to the seller; lemons are worth $5,000 to a buyer and $3,000 to the seller. If buyers could tell the two apart, every car would sell and each trade would create $2,000 of surplus. But buyers can't tell, so they will pay at most the average value of what is on offer. With 50% good cars, this is 0.5 × 10,000 + 0.5 × 5,000 = $7,500. Owners of good cars want at least $8,000, so they pull out; only lemons remain, and then the price falls to $5,000. A good market has been destroyed by bad quality driving it out.

The market survives only if enough cars are good: with a share s of good cars the price is 5,000 + 5,000s, which reaches $8,000 when s = 0.6. Below a 60% share the good cars leave the market.

Principals and agents

When one person (the principal) hires another (the agent) to act on their behalf, such as shareholders and a CEO, or a patient and a doctor, the agent's actions are often hard to see and their interests differ. The principal can monitor the agent, or design a contract that ties pay to results (bonuses, commissions, deductibles, co-payments), so that the agent's incentives line up with the principal's. Pay for performance helps, but if outcomes are noisy it also loads risk on the agent, so there is a trade-off between incentives and insurance.

Signaling and screening

The informed side can reveal its type through a costly signal: a warranty on a used car, a university degree. The signal works only if it costs far less for the good type than for the bad type to send. The uninformed side can screen by offering a menu of options that each type picks differently: insurers offering a low-premium, high-deductible plan alongside a high-premium, full-coverage plan, so that people reveal their risk by their choice.

Health insurance

Insurance markets suffer from adverse selection: people who expect high medical costs buy the most coverage, so premiums rise, so healthier people drop out, and so on. This "death spiral" is one reason the Affordable Care Act combined its ban on denying coverage for pre-existing conditions with a requirement to hold coverage (the individual mandate) and subsidies: to keep healthy people in the pool. The federal penalty for not having coverage was set to zero from 2019, and economists continue to study how the market responded.

Time: present value

A dollar today is worth more than a dollar next year, because it can be invested at an interest rate r. The future value of $1 today after t years is (1 + r)t; the present value (PV) of $1 received in t years is the reverse:

PV = F ÷ (1 + r)t$1,000 in 10 years at 5% is worth 1,000 ÷ 1.0510 ≈ $614 today. A useful shortcut, the rule of 70: money grows at r% a year doubles in about 70 ÷ r years.

Investment decisions: net present value

To evaluate a project that costs money now and pays later, add up the present values of all payments and subtract the cost: net present value (NPV). Undertake projects with a positive NPV. A $10,000 machine that yields $2,600 a year for five years looks profitable (13,000 > 10,000), but at an 8% interest rate the five payments are worth $2,600 × 3.993 = $10,381 today, so NPV ≈ +$381: marginal but positive. At 10% its NPV is negative.

The capital market

Households save (supply funds) and firms invest (demand funds), and the interest rate is the price that balances them. A higher rate means more saving but less investment because fewer projects have a positive NPV. The rate is higher for risky borrowers, and the real rate (interest minus inflation) is the right one for judging real returns.

Key takeaways

  • Adverse selection (hidden type, before the deal) can make markets unravel; moral hazard (hidden action, after the deal) weakens incentives; contracts, signals and screening help.
  • Present value = F ÷ (1 + r)t; the rule of 70 gives doubling time.
  • Undertake a project if its net present value is positive; the interest rate balances saving and investment.
