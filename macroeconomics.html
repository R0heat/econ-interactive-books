Module 1

The Big Questions of Macroeconomics

You will learn to

  • Explain what macroeconomics studies and why it began
  • Name the key indicators: output, jobs, prices, interest rates, debt
  • Distinguish the long run (growth) from the short run (fluctuations)
  • Read today's U.S. economy through those indicators

Microeconomics studies individual markets. Macroeconomics studies the whole economy: why some countries are rich, why recessions happen, and what governments and central banks can do about them.

Born in a crisis

Gregory Mankiw (Harvard), author of the world's best-selling economics textbooks, argues in his 2006 essay "The Macroeconomist as Scientist and Engineer" that the field began not as a pure science but as a kind of engineering, born to solve one practical problem: the Great Depression of the 1930s. John Maynard Keynes's General Theory (1936) offered a diagnosis, too little spending, and a prescription. Ever since, macroeconomists have been both scientists, trying to understand how the economy works, and engineers, trying to fix it. Mankiw's advice to both: be humble and competent.

Two time horizons

The long runWhat determines a country's standard of living? Saving, investment, education, technology and institutions. (Modules 5–8)
The short runWhy does output swing around its trend in recessions and booms, and can policy smooth those swings? (Modules 9–12)

Models and disagreement

Macroeconomists use simplified models: the circular flow, supply and demand for loanable funds, aggregate demand and supply. They disagree more than microeconomists, partly because they can't run experiments on whole economies and partly because policy involves values. Paul Krugman (Princeton and CUNY, Nobel 2008) and Mankiw often land on opposite sides of policy debates, yet they teach nearly the same core models. This book presents that shared core and flags where the debates lie.

Key takeaways

  • Macroeconomics was born to understand and fix the Great Depression.
  • The long run is about growth; the short run is about fluctuations.
  • The key indicators are output, unemployment, inflation, interest rates and public debt.

Module 2

Measuring Output: GDP

You will learn to

  • Define GDP and what it leaves out
  • Use the expenditure approach: Y = C + I + G + NX
  • Separate nominal from real GDP and compute the GDP deflator
  • Discuss GDP as a measure of well-being

Gross domestic product is the market value of all final goods and services produced within a country in a given period.

Reading the definition

  • Market value: goods are added up at their prices.
  • Final goods only: counting flour and the bread made from it would double-count.
  • Produced this period: a used car resold doesn't count; the dealer's service does.
  • Within a country: a Toyota plant in Kentucky counts in U.S. GDP.

Because every dollar spent is a dollar of someone's income, GDP measures both total spending and total income.

The expenditure approach

Y = C + I + G + NXConsumption + investment + government purchases + net exports (exports − imports)
ComponentWhat's in itShare of U.S. GDP (approx.)
Consumption (C)Household spending on goods and services~68%
Investment (I)Business equipment, software, structures, new housing, inventories. Buying stocks is not investment here.~18%
Government purchases (G)Spending on goods, services and public investment. Transfer payments like Social Security aren't included.~17%
Net exports (NX)Exports minus imports~−3%

In Q3 2026 real GDP grew at a 3.4% annual rate, driven by investment, consumer and government spending, partly offset by lower exports; imports, which are subtracted, rose.

Nominal vs real GDP

Nominal GDP uses current prices, so it rises when prices rise even if nothing more is produced. Real GDP values output at constant base-year prices, so it measures quantities. The GDP deflator links the two:

GDP deflator = (nominal GDP ÷ real GDP) × 100  ·  Real GDP = nominal GDP ÷ (deflator / 100)
Worked example. An economy makes only pizzas. In 2025 (the base year) it makes 100 at $10: nominal and real GDP are both $1,000. In 2026 it makes 110 at $12: nominal GDP is $1,320 but real GDP is 110 × $10 = $1,100. The deflator is 120, so prices rose 20% and real output 10%.

Is GDP a good measure of well-being?

GDP per person is strongly linked to health, education and life satisfaction across countries. But it leaves out household production and leisure, the underground economy and environmental damage, and it says nothing about how income is shared. Robert Kennedy said in 1968 that GDP measures everything "except that which makes life worthwhile." Economists use GDP for what it is, a measure of market production, alongside other indicators.

Key takeaways

  • GDP is the market value of final goods and services produced domestically: Y = C + I + G + NX.
  • Real GDP measures quantities; the deflator measures prices.
  • GDP is a good but incomplete gauge of well-being.

  • OpenStax, Principles of Macroeconomics 3e, ch. 6 (free online)
  • J. Stiglitz (Columbia), A. Sen & J.-P. Fitoussi, Mismeasuring Our Lives (2010)
Module 3

Unemployment and the Labor Market

You will learn to

  • Compute the unemployment rate and labor-force participation rate
  • Distinguish frictional, structural and cyclical unemployment
  • Explain the natural rate of unemployment
  • Use Okun's law to link output and jobs

For most people, recessions are felt as lost jobs. Measuring unemployment carefully is where macroeconomics meets daily life.

Counting the unemployed

Each month the Bureau of Labor Statistics surveys about 60,000 households. Adults (16+) are employed, unemployed (without work but actively searching in the last four weeks), or not in the labor force (students, retirees, people not looking).

Unemployment rate = unemployed ÷ labor force  ·  Participation rate = labor force ÷ adult populationLabor force = employed + unemployed

In October 2026, unemployment was 4.1%, with 7.1 million people unemployed, and it had stayed between 4.1% and 4.3% since March. The participation rate was 61.9%. The official rate misses discouraged workers, who have given up searching, and part-timers who want full-time work; the broader U-6 measure includes them.

Three kinds of unemployment

TypeCauseExample
FrictionalTime spent matching workers and jobsA graduate searching for the right first job
StructuralMismatch of skills or location, or wages held above market-clearing (minimum wages, unions, efficiency wages)A coal miner in a region where mines have closed
CyclicalToo little demand in a recessionLayoffs across industries in 2008–09 or spring 2020

The natural rate of unemployment is the frictional plus structural rate, the unemployment that persists even when the economy is at full strength. Estimates for the U.S. are around 4–4.5%. It isn't fixed: better job matching, demographics and policy all move it.

Okun's law

Arthur Okun (Yale, chair of President Johnson's Council of Economic Advisers) found a rule of thumb in 1962: when output falls below its potential, unemployment rises above the natural rate, roughly one point of unemployment for every two points of lost output.

Lab 3.1Okun's law

Set the output gap: how far real GDP is above (+) or below (−) its potential. The natural rate is set at 4.2%.

Key takeaways

  • Unemployment rate = unemployed ÷ labor force; participation matters too.
  • Frictional + structural unemployment = the natural rate; cyclical unemployment comes from recessions.
  • Okun's law: about 1 point of unemployment for every 2 points of output gap.

Sources & further reading

  • BLS, The Employment Situation, October 2026; Current Population Survey
  • A. Okun, "Potential GNP: Its Measurement and Significance" (1962)
Module 4

Inflation and the Cost of Living

You will learn to

  • Build and use the Consumer Price Index
  • Compare the CPI with the PCE price index and GDP deflator
  • Convert dollars across years
  • Explain the costs of inflation and deflation

Inflation is a general rise in prices, a fall in what each dollar buys.

The Consumer Price Index

The BLS prices a fixed basket of goods and services bought by a typical urban household (housing, food, transportation, medical care and more), compares its cost with a base period (1982–84 = 100), and reports the index each month. The inflation rate is the percentage change in the index, usually over 12 months.

Inflation = (CPI this year − CPI last year) ÷ CPI last year × 100

A measurement lesson from 2026

In October 2026 the CPI rose 0.4%, but BLS didn't report the usual 12-month rate: the October 2025 government shutdown had disrupted data collection, so there was no year-earlier index to compare. Private analysts estimated annual inflation of roughly 4% by other methods. The episode shows that official statistics depend on people and budgets.

Three price measures

IndexCoversUsed for
CPIFixed basket of consumer purchasesIndexing Social Security, tax brackets and many contracts
PCE price indexAll consumer spending, with weights updated as people switch productsThe Fed's 2% inflation target
GDP deflatorEverything produced domesticallyConverting nominal to real GDP

The CPI tends to overstate the rise in the cost of living: it misses substitution toward cheaper goods, quality improvements and new products. The 1996 Boskin Commission estimated the bias at about 1.1 points a year, and BLS has since reduced it.

Comparing dollars across time

Price in today's dollars = old price × (CPI today ÷ CPI then)

Why inflation is costly

  • Unexpected inflation redistributes from lenders to borrowers and from people on fixed incomes.
  • Shoe-leather and menu costs: people hold less cash, and firms must keep re-pricing.
  • Relative-price confusion and tax distortions, since taxes hit nominal gains.
  • Uncertainty makes long-term planning harder.

Deflation is dangerous too. Falling prices raise the real burden of debt, and expected deflation makes people postpone spending. Irving Fisher (Yale) described this "debt-deflation" spiral during the Great Depression.

Key takeaways

  • The CPI measures the cost of a fixed basket; the Fed targets 2% PCE inflation.
  • Use CPI ratios to convert dollars across years.
  • Unexpected inflation redistributes wealth; deflation raises real debt burdens.

Sources & further reading

  • BLS, Consumer Price Index; CNBC, "Here's the inflation breakdown for October 2026" (Nov 10, 2026)
  • I. Fisher, "The Debt-Deflation Theory of Great Depressions," Econometrica (1933)
Module 5

Long-Run Growth: Solow and Romer

You will learn to

  • Use the rule of 70 to see the power of compound growth
  • Explain diminishing returns to capital and the Solow steady state
  • Explain why ideas, being nonrival, can sustain growth (Romer)
  • Identify policies and institutions that promote growth

"Once one starts to think about [growth], it is hard to think about anything else." — Robert Lucas (Nobel 1995)

The arithmetic of growth

Small differences in growth rates compound into huge differences in living standards. The rule of 70: a quantity growing at g% a year doubles in about 70/g years. At 1% growth, income doubles every 70 years; at 3%, every 23.

The Solow model

Robert Solow (MIT, Nobel 1987) asked what saving and investment can do for growth. Output per worker depends on capital per worker, with diminishing returns: each extra machine adds less. Each year, investment (a share s of output) adds capital, while depreciation and population growth wear it down or spread it thinner. Capital per worker rises until the two balance at a steady state.

Steady state: s · f(k) = (δ + n) · kInvestment per worker = the investment needed just to keep capital per worker constant

Three lessons follow. (1) A higher saving rate raises the level of income but not its long-run growth rate. (2) Poorer countries with less capital should grow faster and catch up, called convergence, if they share the same fundamentals. (3) Sustained growth in income per person must come from technological progress. Solow found that most U.S. growth couldn't be explained by more capital and labor; the leftover is called total factor productivity.

Lab 5.1The Solow model

Output per worker is y = A·k1/3. Change the saving rate, depreciation plus population growth, and technology.

Romer: growth from ideas

Paul Romer (NYU, Nobel 2018) made technology endogenous, produced inside the economy by profit-seeking research. The key is that ideas are nonrival: a recipe, a formula or a piece of code can be used by everyone at once without being used up, unlike a machine. So the stock of ideas can grow without running into diminishing returns, and growth can continue indefinitely. But because inventors can't capture all the benefits of their ideas, markets underinvest in research, which justifies patents, research funding and education. Romer's model, published in 1990, is the foundation of endogenous growth theory.

Institutions

Why don't poor countries simply copy rich ones' ideas? Research by Daron Acemoglu (MIT) and James Robinson (Chicago), with Simon Johnson (MIT), all three Nobel laureates in 2024, points to institutions: secure property rights, rule of law and inclusive politics give people the incentive to save, invest and innovate.

Key takeaways

  • Rule of 70: small growth differences compound into big income gaps.
  • In Solow's model, saving raises the level of income; only technology sustains growth.
  • Nonrival ideas (Romer) and good institutions drive long-run prosperity.

Sources & further reading

  • Nobel Prize Outreach, "Integrating nature and knowledge into economics" (2018)
  • C. Jones (Stanford), "Paul Romer: Ideas, Nonrivalry, and Endogenous Growth", Scand. J. Econ. (2019)
  • R. Solow, "A Contribution to the Theory of Economic Growth," QJE (1956)
Module 6

Saving, Investment and the Financial System

You will learn to

  • Derive the identity: national saving = investment (closed economy)
  • Split saving into private and public saving
  • Use the market for loanable funds
  • Explain how deficits can crowd out investment

Growth needs investment, and investment must be financed by someone's saving. The financial system connects the two.

The saving identity

In a closed economy, Y = C + I + G. Rearranging gives national saving: what's left after consumption and government purchases.

S = Y − C − G = I  ·  S = (Y − T − C) + (T − G)Private saving + public saving. When T < G, the government runs a deficit and public saving is negative.

The market for loanable funds

Savers supply loanable funds and investors demand them; the real interest rate balances the two. Policies that encourage saving (tax-advantaged retirement accounts) shift supply right and lower rates; policies that encourage investment (investment tax credits) shift demand right and raise rates.

Deficits and crowding out

A government deficit reduces national saving: the supply of loanable funds shifts left, the interest rate rises, and private investment falls. This is crowding out. Over time, less investment means a smaller capital stock and lower future income. In fiscal 2026 the U.S. federal deficit was $2.0 trillion, 6.2% of GDP, well above the 50-year average of 3.8%.

Lab 6.1Deficits and crowding out

Raise the government deficit (which reduces national saving) or change firms' investment demand. Dashed lines show the starting curves.

The debate. Crowding out is strongest when the economy is at full employment. Krugman has argued that in a deep slump with interest rates near zero, as in 2009–2015, government borrowing crowds out little because idle savings are plentiful. Mankiw and others stress the long-run cost of high debt. Both views fit the model; they disagree about which conditions apply.

Key takeaways

  • National saving = private + public saving = investment (closed economy).
  • The real interest rate balances the supply of and demand for loanable funds.
  • Deficits reduce saving, raise rates and crowd out investment, most strongly at full employment.

Module 7

Money and Inflation in the Long Run

You will learn to

  • Describe the functions of money and how central banks supply it
  • Use the quantity equation MV = PY
  • Explain the classical dichotomy and monetary neutrality
  • Explain hyperinflation and the Fisher effect

Over the long run, the most reliable fact in macroeconomics is that countries that print money fast get high inflation.

Money

Money is a medium of exchange, a unit of account and a store of value. Modern money is fiat money plus bank deposits, managed by a central bank (the Federal Reserve in the U.S.). The companion volume Money and Banking covers this in depth.

The quantity theory

M × V = P × YMoney × velocity = price level × real output. In growth rates: π ≈ μ + (velocity growth) − (real output growth)

If velocity is roughly stable and real output is set by labor, capital and technology (Module 5), faster money growth translates into faster inflation. This is the classical dichotomy: in the long run, money affects nominal variables (prices, wages, nominal interest rates) but not real ones (output, employment, real interest rates). Money is neutral in the long run, though almost all economists agree it is not neutral in the short run (Modules 9–11).

Lab 7.1The quantity theory in growth rates

Choose money growth, velocity growth and real output growth. Inflation is what's left over.

Hyperinflation

When governments can't borrow or tax enough, they print money to pay bills. Germany in 1923, Zimbabwe in 2008 and Venezuela in the late 2010s saw prices doubling in days or weeks. The inflation tax falls on everyone holding money. Thomas Sargent (Nobel 2011) showed that such inflations end abruptly when a credible fiscal and monetary reform stops the printing.

The Fisher effect

Irving Fisher (Yale) noted that nominal interest rate = real rate + expected inflation. In the long run, when money growth raises inflation one-for-one, nominal interest rates rise one-for-one too. That's why high-inflation countries have high interest rates.

Key takeaways

  • MV = PY: in the long run, money growth drives inflation.
  • Money is neutral in the long run but not the short run.
  • Hyperinflations come from printing money to finance deficits; nominal rates track inflation (Fisher).

Sources & further reading

  • M. Friedman & A. Schwartz, A Monetary History of the United States (1963)
  • T. Sargent, "The Ends of Four Big Inflations" (1982)
Module 8

The Open Economy

You will learn to

  • Link net exports to saving and investment: NX = S − I
  • Read nominal and real exchange rates
  • Apply purchasing power parity
  • Discuss trade deficits and tariffs as macro issues

No economy is an island. Goods and capital flow across borders, and the two flows are mirror images.

The key identity

S − I = NX = net capital outflowA country that saves less than it invests must borrow the difference from abroad, and runs a trade deficit.

The U.S. has run trade deficits for decades because national saving (including a large government deficit) falls short of investment, and foreigners are eager to hold U.S. assets. A trade deficit is not, by itself, a sign of weakness or of "unfair" trade: it's the counterpart of a capital inflow. Tariffs can change which goods are traded, but most economists, Mankiw and Krugman included, agree they don't fix an overall deficit unless they change saving or investment.

Exchange rates

The nominal exchange rate is the price of one currency in another (for example, euros per dollar). The real exchange rate adjusts for price levels: it's the rate at which a country's goods trade for foreign goods. A stronger real exchange rate makes exports more expensive abroad and imports cheaper at home.

Real exchange rate = nominal rate × (domestic price ÷ foreign price)

Purchasing power parity

Purchasing power parity (PPP) says a dollar should buy the same basket everywhere; otherwise traders would buy where it's cheap and sell where it's dear. PPP fails in the short run (many goods aren't traded, and capital flows swamp trade flows) but is a good long-run anchor. The Economist's Big Mac Index is a lighthearted test.

Key takeaways

  • NX = S − I: trade deficits mirror capital inflows.
  • The real exchange rate drives net exports.
  • PPP holds roughly in the long run, not the short run.

Sources & further reading

  • P. Krugman, M. Obstfeld & M. Melitz, International Economics (standard text)
  • N. G. Mankiw, columns on trade deficits, mankiw.scholars.harvard.edu
Module 9

Aggregate Demand and Aggregate Supply

You will learn to

  • Explain why aggregate demand slopes down
  • Distinguish short-run and long-run aggregate supply
  • Analyze demand shocks and supply shocks
  • Explain how the economy self-corrects, and why that can be slow

Recessions are short-run departures from the economy's long-run path. The AD–AS model is the standard tool for analyzing them.

Aggregate demand

The AD curve shows the quantity of output demanded at each price level. It slopes down because a lower price level raises real wealth, lowers interest rates (people need less money, so they lend more) and makes exports cheaper. AD shifts with anything that changes spending at a given price level: consumer confidence, investment, government purchases, taxes, the money supply, and foreign demand.

Aggregate supply

In the long run, output equals potential output, set by labor, capital and technology: the LRAS curve is vertical. In the short run, the SRAS curve slopes up because some wages and prices are sticky. Mankiw's research on "menu costs" (1985) explains why even small costs of changing prices can produce large swings in output. Misperceptions and long-term contracts add to the stickiness. SRAS shifts with expected prices, input costs (oil) and productivity.

Lab 9.1Shocks in the AD–AS model

Hit the economy with a demand shock or a supply shock (positive = higher costs, like an oil price spike). Then tick "Long run" to let wages and price expectations adjust.

Two kinds of recession

A demand shock, such as the collapse in spending in 2008–09, lowers both output and prices. A supply shock, like the 1970s oil embargoes or the 2021–22 supply-chain and energy disruptions, raises prices while lowering output: stagflation. Supply shocks present policymakers with a dilemma: stimulating demand worsens inflation, and restraining it deepens the slump.

Self-correction and its limits

In the model, a demand slump eventually cures itself: with output below potential, wages and price expectations fall, SRAS shifts right, and output returns to potential at a lower price level. But that can take years, and falling prices raise debt burdens. As Keynes put it, "in the long run we are all dead." Whether to wait or act is the central question of stabilization policy.

Key takeaways

  • AD slopes down; LRAS is vertical at potential output; SRAS slopes up because prices are sticky.
  • Demand shocks move output and prices together; supply shocks move them in opposite directions.
  • The economy self-corrects, but slowly, which creates a case for policy.

Sources & further reading

  • N. G. Mankiw, "Small Menu Costs and Large Business Cycles," QJE (1985)
  • OpenStax, Principles of Macroeconomics 3e, ch. 11 (The Aggregate Demand/Aggregate Supply Model)
Module 10

Keynesian Economics and the Multiplier

You will learn to

  • Explain Krugman's baby-sitting co-op parable
  • Use the Keynesian cross to find equilibrium output
  • Compute the spending and tax multipliers
  • Explain the paradox of thrift

Keynes's central idea: in the short run, total spending determines total output. If everyone tries to spend less at once, incomes fall.

The baby-sitting co-op

Paul Krugman's favorite parable comes from a 1977 article by Joan and Richard Sweeney. About 150 young couples on Capitol Hill traded baby-sitting using scrip: coupons worth half an hour of sitting. Many couples wanted to build up a reserve of coupons, so they tried to baby-sit more and go out less. But one couple's night out is another's chance to earn, so baby-sitting jobs dried up, couples grew even more reluctant to spend coupons, and the co-op fell into a recession. Rules requiring members to go out didn't help. Issuing more coupons, a monetary expansion, did. Krugman's 1998 essay "Baby-Sitting the Economy" uses the story to explain recessions as shortfalls of demand, and to ask what happens when even cheap money doesn't revive spending, as in Japan in the 1990s.

The Keynesian cross

Planned expenditure (AE) = consumption + planned investment + government purchases + net exports. Consumption depends on income: C = C₀ + MPC × Y, where the marginal propensity to consume (MPC) is the share of each extra dollar of income that's spent. Equilibrium is where planned spending equals output, on the 45° line:

Y = A + MPC × Y  →  Y* = A ÷ (1 − MPC)A = autonomous spending (C₀ + I + G + NX)
Lab 10.1The Keynesian cross

Autonomous spending starts at $200 billion. Change government purchases and the MPC; equilibrium is where spending crosses the 45° line.

The multiplier

A $1 increase in government purchases becomes someone's income; they spend MPC × $1, which becomes another's income, and so on: 1 + MPC + MPC² + … = 1/(1 − MPC). With MPC = 0.75 the spending multiplier is 4. A tax cut works through consumption only, so the tax multiplier is smaller: −MPC/(1 − MPC).

In practice multipliers are much smaller than the simple formula suggests, because of taxes, imports, crowding out and monetary policy reactions. Evidence collected by Christina Romer (Berkeley, chair of President Obama's Council of Economic Advisers) and others puts fiscal multipliers mostly between about 0.5 and 2: larger in deep slumps with interest rates stuck at zero, smaller near full employment.

The paradox of thrift

Saving is virtuous for one family, but if everyone tries to save more at once, spending and incomes fall, and total saving may not rise at all. That's exactly what happened in the baby-sitting co-op.

Key takeaways

  • In the short run, spending determines output: Y* = A/(1 − MPC).
  • Spending multiplier = 1/(1 − MPC); the tax multiplier is smaller.
  • Real-world multipliers are smaller than the textbook formula, and largest in deep slumps.

Sources & further reading

  • P. Krugman, "Baby-Sitting the Economy", Slate (Aug. 13, 1998)
  • J. Sweeney & R. Sweeney, "Monetary Theory and the Great Capitol Hill Baby Sitting Co-op Crisis," JMCB (1977)
  • J. M. Keynes, The General Theory of Employment, Interest and Money (1936)
Module 11

Monetary and Fiscal Policy

You will learn to

  • Explain how the Fed uses interest rates to steer aggregate demand
  • Describe the liquidity trap and unconventional policy
  • Distinguish discretionary fiscal policy from automatic stabilizers
  • Analyze government debt dynamics

Two levers manage aggregate demand: the central bank's interest rate and the government's budget.

Monetary policy

The Federal Reserve sets a target for the overnight federal funds rate. Lower rates encourage borrowing for homes, cars and investment, lift asset prices and weaken the dollar, all of which shift AD right. Higher rates do the reverse. The Fed has a dual mandate: maximum employment and 2% inflation. In September 2026 it raised its target range to 3.75%–4.00%, its first increase since 2023, as inflation stayed above target. A useful guide is the Taylor rule (John Taylor, Stanford): raise the rate more than one-for-one with inflation, and lean against output gaps.

The liquidity trap

What if the economy needs a negative interest rate? Nominal rates can't go much below zero, since people can hold cash instead. In 1998 Krugman revived Keynes's idea of a liquidity trap to analyze Japan: once rates hit zero, adding money no longer stimulates spending. His prescription was for the central bank to credibly promise future inflation, lowering real rates. After 2008 the Fed, the ECB and the Bank of Japan used forward guidance and quantitative easing (buying long-term bonds), and in that setting Krugman argued that fiscal expansion becomes especially powerful.

Fiscal policy

Discretionary fiscal policy means deliberate changes in spending or taxes, such as the 2009 Recovery Act or the 2020–21 pandemic relief. It faces long lags (legislation, implementation) and political constraints. Automatic stabilizers, like income taxes that fall and unemployment benefits that rise in a recession, kick in without new legislation and are fast. Most economists see monetary policy as the first line of defense and fiscal policy as essential when monetary policy is constrained.

Government debt

The debt ratio's path depends on the primary deficit (the deficit excluding interest) and the gap between the interest rate on the debt (r) and the economy's growth rate (g):

Change in debt/GDP ≈ (r − g) × debt/GDP + primary deficit/GDP

When r < g, as for much of 2010–2021, a country can run modest primary deficits with a stable debt ratio, a point emphasized by Olivier Blanchard (MIT, former IMF chief economist). When r > g, debt compounds. U.S. federal debt held by the public reached 100% of GDP in fiscal 2026, the highest since 1946, and CBO's February 2026 outlook projected it rising to 120% by 2036.

Key takeaways

  • The Fed moves AD through interest rates; at the zero lower bound it relies on guidance and QE.
  • Automatic stabilizers act fast; discretionary fiscal policy acts with lags.
  • Debt dynamics hinge on r − g and the primary deficit.

Module 12

Inflation and Unemployment: The Phillips Curve

You will learn to

  • Describe the original Phillips curve
  • Explain Friedman and Phelps's natural-rate critique
  • Use the expectations-augmented Phillips curve
  • Interpret the Volcker disinflation and the 2021–23 inflation

Is there a trade-off between inflation and unemployment? Yes in the short run, no in the long run, and how expectations behave decides the rest.

The original curve

In 1958 A. W. Phillips found an inverse relation between wage inflation and unemployment in Britain. Paul Samuelson and Robert Solow (both MIT Nobel laureates) showed the same pattern in U.S. data in 1960 and suggested policymakers could choose a point on the menu: a bit more inflation for a bit less unemployment.

Friedman and Phelps

In 1967–68 Milton Friedman and Edmund Phelps (Columbia, Nobel 2006) argued that the menu was an illusion. Workers and firms care about real wages; once they expect higher inflation, they build it into wage and price setting. Unemployment then returns to its natural rate with permanently higher inflation. The long-run Phillips curve is vertical. The 1970s proved them right: inflation and unemployment rose together.

π = πᵉ − β (u − u*) + supply shockInflation = expected inflation − β × (unemployment − natural rate) + supply shocks

Volcker's disinflation

By 1980 U.S. inflation exceeded 13%. Fed Chair Paul Volcker tightened sharply; unemployment rose above 10% in 1982 and inflation fell to about 4% by 1983. The costs were large but temporary, and the episode showed that credibility, changing expectations, is central. Since the 1990s anchored expectations helped keep inflation near 2%.

The 2021–23 surge and after

Pandemic-era supply disruptions, energy shocks and strong demand pushed CPI inflation above 9% in mid-2022. Economists divided: some, like Larry Summers (Harvard), warned early that stimulus would overheat the economy; others saw mainly temporary supply shocks. Both turned out partly right. The Fed raised rates sharply in 2022–23, and inflation fell without a recession, aided by healing supply chains and anchored expectations. In 2026 inflation is again above target, and the Fed raised rates in September. Whether expectations stay anchored is the question every central bank is watching.

Key takeaways

  • There's a short-run inflation–unemployment trade-off, but no long-run one: the long-run Phillips curve is vertical at the natural rate.
  • Expected inflation shifts the short-run curve; supply shocks shift it too.
  • Credibility and anchored expectations make disinflation cheaper.
