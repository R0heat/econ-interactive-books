Module 1

What Is Money?

You will learn to

  • Name the three functions of money
  • Trace money from commodities to fiat currency and bank deposits
  • Explain why money rests on trust, using the island of Yap
  • Describe the "hierarchy of money" and the measures M1 and M2

Money is whatever people generally accept in payment. That simple definition hides a surprising amount of economics.

Three jobs money does

Medium of exchangeIt lets us skip barter, which needs a "double coincidence of wants": the baker must want exactly what the shoemaker has.
Unit of accountIt's the common measuring stick for prices. With 1,000 goods and no money you'd need 499,500 relative prices; with money, 1,000.
Store of valueIt carries purchasing power into the future, though inflation erodes it, and other assets (bonds, houses) store value too.
LiquidityMoney is the most liquid asset: it can be spent immediately at full value. Other assets must first be converted.

Economists use "money" more narrowly than everyday speech. Income is a flow (dollars per year); wealth is a stock of all assets; money is the part of wealth used for payments.

From shells to screens

Societies have used cattle, salt, shells, tobacco and, above all, gold and silver as commodity money, which has value in itself. Paper notes began as claims on metal held in vaults. Today's dollars, euros and yen are fiat money: not convertible into anything, accepted because the government declares them legal tender and, more importantly, because everyone expects everyone else to accept them. Most money now isn't paper at all but bank deposits moved by checks, cards and electronic transfers, and new forms keep appearing, from mobile wallets to stablecoins.

The island of stone money

Milton Friedman (Nobel 1976) liked to tell the story of Yap, a Pacific island where large carved stone wheels, some twelve feet across, served as money. Owners rarely moved them; ownership simply changed by common agreement. One huge stone was said to have sunk during a storm while being shipped, yet it stayed part of a family's wealth because everyone agreed it existed. Friedman's point, in his 1991 essay "The Island of Stone Money," was that modern systems work the same way: in 1932, when the Federal Reserve Bank of New York "transferred" gold to the Bank of France merely by earmarking it within its own vaults, markets treated it as a real shift in wealth. Money is a social agreement backed by trust.

The hierarchy of money

Perry Mehrling, who taught money and banking at Barnard College, Columbia, for three decades, stresses that monetary systems are hierarchical. Your bank deposit is a promise by your bank to pay you central-bank money. Your bank's reserves are a deposit at the central bank. Across borders, dollars and gold sit at the top. Each layer is a promise to pay something higher up. In calm times the layers trade at par and look identical; in a crisis people scramble up the hierarchy toward safer money, which is why "liquidity" becomes the central issue in every financial panic.

LevelWhose promise?Settles in…
Gold / world reserve currencyNo one's (or the U.S.)— (top of the hierarchy)
Central-bank money (currency and reserves)The central bankInternational reserves
Bank depositsA commercial bankCentral-bank reserves
Securities, IOUs, trade creditFirms, governments, householdsBank deposits

Measuring money

The Federal Reserve publishes two main aggregates. M1 is the most liquid: currency held by the public, demand deposits, and (since 2020, when the Fed reclassified them) savings deposits. M2 adds small time deposits and retail money-market fund shares. The monetary base (currency plus bank reserves) is the central bank's own liability, the foundation the rest is built on (Module 10).

Key takeaways

  • Money is a medium of exchange, unit of account and store of value.
  • Modern money is fiat money and bank deposits, and it rests on trust.
  • Money is hierarchical: lower layers are promises to pay in higher, safer ones.

Sources & further reading

Module 2

The Financial System

You will learn to

  • Distinguish direct from indirect finance
  • Classify financial markets and intermediaries
  • Explain adverse selection, moral hazard and credit rationing
  • Explain why banks exist, following Diamond's theory of delegated monitoring

The financial system moves funds from people who have saved to people with productive uses for them, and moves risk to those best able to bear it.

Direct and indirect finance

In direct finance, borrowers sell securities (stocks and bonds) straight to savers in financial markets. In indirect finance, a financial intermediary such as a bank, insurer, pension fund or mutual fund stands in between: it takes in savers' funds and lends or invests them. Indirect finance is far larger, especially for households and small firms.

MarketWhat trades
Debt vs equityFixed promises to pay (bonds, loans) vs ownership claims (stocks)
Primary vs secondaryNew issues sold by the borrower vs resale of existing securities
Money vs capital marketShort-term debt (under a year: T-bills, commercial paper, repo) vs long-term debt and equity
Exchanges vs over-the-counterCentral trading venue vs dealer networks (most bonds trade OTC)

Why intermediaries?

Transaction costs: pooling many small savings and making many loans is far cheaper per dollar. Risk sharing: intermediaries hold diversified portfolios and transform risky assets into safer ones for savers. Liquidity: a deposit can be withdrawn on demand even though it funds a 30-year mortgage (Module 8). And above all, information.

Asymmetric information

Borrowers know more about their own projects than lenders do. George Akerlof (Nobel 2001) showed in "The Market for Lemons" (1970) how this can unravel a market: if buyers can't tell good used cars from bad, they pay only an average price, so owners of good cars withdraw them, and the average gets worse. In finance this is adverse selection: the borrowers most eager for a loan are often the riskiest. After the loan is made comes moral hazard: the borrower may take risks the lender wouldn't approve of.

Joseph Stiglitz (Columbia, Nobel 2001) and Andrew Weiss showed in 1981 that lenders may respond with credit rationing. Rather than raising interest rates, which would drive away safe borrowers and attract gamblers, they limit loans even to borrowers willing to pay more. Remedies include collateral, net worth (borrowers with more to lose behave better), covenants, credit scores, and specialized lenders who build up knowledge.

Why banks exist: delegated monitoring

Douglas Diamond (Nobel 2022, PhD Yale) explained why banks are a natural solution. Checking on borrowers is costly. If thousands of small savers each monitored each borrower, effort would be wasted. A bank can monitor on everyone's behalf, which reduces bankruptcies and costs to society. And depositors don't need to monitor the bank itself, because a bank that neglects monitoring faces losses that would leave it unable to repay them. By lending to many borrowers, the bank also diversifies, keeping losses small and predictable.

Key takeaways

  • Finance is direct (markets) or indirect (intermediaries); indirect is larger.
  • Adverse selection and moral hazard can shut down lending; credit rationing is one response.
  • Banks economize on monitoring, diversify risk, and create liquidity.

Sources & further reading

Module 3

Interest Rates and Present Value

You will learn to

  • Compute present and future values
  • Price a coupon bond and find its yield to maturity
  • Explain why bond prices and yields move in opposite directions
  • Separate nominal from real interest rates (the Fisher equation)

A dollar today is worth more than a dollar next year. Present value is the tool that makes money at different dates comparable.

Present value

At interest rate i, $100 today grows to $100(1 + i)ⁿ in n years. Turn it around: a payment CF received in n years is worth today

PV = CF ÷ (1 + i)ⁿ$1,000 due in 10 years is worth $1,000 ÷ 1.05¹⁰ ≈ $614 today at 5%.

Four kinds of debt

  • Simple loan: borrow now, repay principal plus interest at maturity.
  • Fixed-payment loan: equal payments covering interest and principal (mortgages, car loans).
  • Coupon bond: fixed coupon payments each year, then the face value at maturity.
  • Discount bond: bought below face value, pays face value at maturity, no coupons (Treasury bills).

Bond prices and yields

A bond's price is the present value of its payments. The yield to maturity is the single interest rate that makes that present value equal the price. It follows that bond prices and yields move in opposite directions: when market rates rise, existing bonds paying old, lower coupons become less valuable. A bond priced at face value has a yield equal to its coupon rate. Longer bonds swing more in price for the same change in rates: this is interest-rate risk.

Lab 3.1Bond prices and yields

A bond with a $1,000 face value. Change its coupon, its maturity and the market yield. The curve shows the price at every yield.

Yield is not the same as return

Your return over a year is the coupon plus any capital gain or loss, divided by what you paid. If rates jump while you hold a long bond, its price falls and your return can be negative even though its yield to maturity was positive. Only when the holding period equals the time to maturity is the return locked in. This is exactly what hurt Silicon Valley Bank in 2022–23 (Module 8).

Real and nominal rates

Irving Fisher, the great Yale economist, separated the nominal interest rate (what's quoted) from the real interest rate (what you earn in purchasing power):

real rate ≈ nominal rate − expected inflationThe Fisher equation. A 5% loan with 3% expected inflation has a real rate of about 2%.

Real rates are what matter for decisions to save, borrow and invest. When they are negative, as they were for much of 2021–22, savers lose purchasing power. Treasury Inflation-Protected Securities (TIPS) pay a real yield directly, so comparing them with ordinary Treasuries reveals the market's expected inflation.

Key takeaways

  • PV = CF ÷ (1 + i)ⁿ; the yield to maturity sets the PV of a bond's payments equal to its price.
  • Prices and yields move inversely; long bonds carry more interest-rate risk.
  • Real rate ≈ nominal rate − expected inflation.

Sources & further reading

  • Robert Shiller, ECON 252: Financial Markets, Open Yale Courses (2011)
  • Irving Fisher, The Theory of Interest (1930)
Module 4

The Behavior of Interest Rates

You will learn to

  • List what determines how much of an asset people want to hold
  • Use the market for loanable funds to explain interest-rate movements
  • Explain the Fisher effect
  • Use Keynes's liquidity preference framework and its limits

Interest rates are prices, so supply and demand explain them. The trick is to identify what's being supplied and demanded.

What makes an asset attractive

How much of an asset people want to hold depends on: wealth (more wealth, more of most assets), expected return relative to other assets, risk (people are generally risk-averse), and liquidity (easy-to-sell assets are prized). This is the theory of portfolio choice.

The market for loanable funds

Think of bonds as loans. People who buy bonds supply loanable funds; firms and governments that issue bonds demand them. The interest rate settles where the two balance.

More wealth / savingMore funds supplied↓
Bonds become riskier or less liquidFewer funds supplied to bonds↑
Larger government deficitsMore bonds issued↑

Liquidity preference

John Maynard Keynes looked at the same question from the money side. People hold money for transactions and safety, but money pays little or no interest, so the interest rate is the opportunity cost of holding it. Money demand slopes down against the interest rate and rises with income and the price level. When the central bank changes the supply of money, the interest rate moves: more money, lower rates (the liquidity effect).

Lab 4.2Liquidity preference

The central bank sets the money supply (the vertical line). Income shifts money demand.

But Milton Friedman warned that the liquidity effect is only the first round. Faster money growth raises income, then prices, then expected inflation. Each raises interest rates. If expectations adjust quickly, easier money can end up with higher interest rates. That's why countries with high money growth have high interest rates, not low ones.

Key takeaways

  • Interest rates are set by the supply of and demand for loanable funds (bonds).
  • Higher expected inflation raises nominal rates (the Fisher effect).
  • More money lowers rates at first, but income, price and expectation effects can reverse that.

Sources & further reading

  • J. M. Keynes, The General Theory (1936), ch. 13–15
  • M. Friedman, "The Role of Monetary Policy," AER (1968)
  • F. Mishkin (Columbia), The Economics of Money, Banking and Financial Markets, the standard text for this framework
Module 5

Risk and the Term Structure of Interest Rates

You will learn to

  • Explain default-risk, liquidity and tax spreads
  • Read a yield curve
  • Compare the expectations, segmented-markets and liquidity-premium theories
  • Explain why an inverted yield curve signals recession

Why do different bonds pay different rates? Two reasons: who owes the money, and for how long.

The risk structure

Default risk: investors require a risk premium to hold bonds that might not pay. U.S. Treasuries are the benchmark "risk-free" asset; corporate bonds pay more, and credit-rating agencies (Moody's, S&P, Fitch) grade them. Bonds rated BBB or above are investment grade; below that they're high-yield ("junk"). Spreads widen sharply in crises, in a "flight to quality." Liquidity matters too: harder-to-sell bonds pay more. And taxes: interest on U.S. municipal bonds is exempt from federal tax, so "munis" can pay less than Treasuries.

The term structure

A yield curve plots yields on bonds with the same risk but different maturities. Usually it slopes upward. Sometimes it's flat, and occasionally inverted (short rates above long rates). Three facts need explaining: (1) rates on different maturities move together; (2) when short rates are low, the curve tends to slope up, and when they're high, it tends to invert; (3) it usually slopes up.

  • Expectations theory: a long rate is the average of expected future short rates. It explains facts 1 and 2 but predicts a flat curve on average, contradicting fact 3.
  • Segmented markets theory: each maturity is its own market. It explains fact 3 but not facts 1 and 2.
  • Liquidity premium theory: a long rate equals the average expected short rate plus a term premium that grows with maturity, because long bonds carry more interest-rate risk. This explains all three.
Lab 5.1Build a yield curve

Choose today's 1-year rate, how short rates are expected to change each year, and the term premium. The dashed curve is pure expectations.

The yield curve as a forecaster

Because long rates embed expectations, an inverted curve signals that markets expect short rates to fall, usually because the central bank will need to cut in a downturn. Inversions have preceded most U.S. recessions since the 1960s. They aren't foolproof: the curve inverted deeply in 2022–23, yet no recession followed immediately, a reminder that term premiums and central-bank bond buying also move long rates.

Key takeaways

  • Spreads reflect default risk, liquidity and taxes.
  • The liquidity premium theory, with expected short rates plus a term premium, best explains yield curves.
  • Inverted curves signal expected rate cuts and often recessions.

Sources & further reading

  • Robert Shiller, ECON 252, lectures on debt markets and the term structure
  • A. Estrella & F. Mishkin, "The Yield Curve as a Predictor of U.S. Recessions," Current Issues in Economics and Finance (FRBNY, 1996)
Module 6

The Stock Market and Efficient Markets

You will learn to

  • Value a stock with the dividend discount (Gordon growth) model
  • State the efficient markets hypothesis and its implications
  • Explain Shiller's excess volatility evidence and behavioral finance
  • Discuss bubbles

A stock is a claim on a company's future profits. Its price is a bet on a future no one can see.

Valuing a stock

A share's value is the present value of all its future dividends. If dividends grow at a steady rate g and investors require a return k, this collapses to the Gordon growth model:

P₀ = D₁ ÷ (k − g)Next year's dividend divided by (required return − growth). Valid only when k > g.

Notice how sensitive this is. With D₁ = $2, k = 8% and g = 3%, the price is $40. If investors start expecting 4% growth, it's $50: a 25% jump from a one-point change in expectations. Small shifts in sentiment about growth or risk can move prices a lot.

Lab 6.1The Gordon growth model

Next year's dividend is $2. Change the expected growth rate and the return investors require.

Efficient markets

The efficient markets hypothesis, associated with Eugene Fama (Chicago, Nobel 2013), holds that prices fully reflect all available information. If they didn't, someone could profit by trading on that information, and in doing so would push the price to where it should be. Implications: past prices can't predict future returns; a stock tip that's already public is worthless; most active fund managers fail to beat low-cost index funds after fees. The evidence on that last point is strong.

Excess volatility and behavioral finance

Robert Shiller (Yale, Nobel 2013, shared with Fama and Lars Hansen) showed in 1981 that stock prices swing far more than the dividends they're supposed to forecast: excess volatility. He argued that psychology (overconfidence, extrapolating recent trends, herd behavior, stories that spread like epidemics) drives prices away from fundamentals for long stretches. His book Irrational Exuberance warned of the dot-com bubble in 2000 and, in its second edition, the housing bubble. Over a few days, prices are hard to predict, consistent with Fama; over years, valuations do predict returns, consistent with Shiller. That is why the Nobel committee could honor both.

Bubbles

A bubble is a price rise driven by expectations of further rises rather than fundamentals. Bubbles financed by credit are the most dangerous: when they burst, borrowers default and lenders' capital vanishes. That is what turned the 2006 housing decline into the 2008 crisis (Module 8), whereas the dot-com crash, financed mostly by equity, caused a milder recession.

Key takeaways

  • Stock price = PV of expected dividends; P = D₁/(k − g) is very sensitive to growth and required return.
  • Markets are hard to beat in the short run (Fama), but prices can stray from fundamentals for long periods (Shiller).
  • Credit-fueled bubbles are the most dangerous.

Sources & further reading

  • Robert Shiller, ECON 252, lectures on efficient markets vs excess volatility and behavioral finance; Irrational Exuberance (2000, 3rd ed. 2015)
  • R. Shiller, "Do Stock Prices Move Too Much to Be Justified by Subsequent Changes in Dividends?" AER (1981); E. Fama, "Efficient Capital Markets," Journal of Finance (1970)
Module 7

Banks: Balance Sheets and Management

You will learn to

  • Read a bank balance sheet and use T-accounts
  • Explain liquidity management and capital adequacy
  • Compute leverage, return on assets and return on equity
  • See how losses and withdrawals threaten a bank

A bank borrows short (deposits) and lends long (loans). Everything about bank management follows from that mismatch.

The bank balance sheet

Assets (uses of funds)Liabilities + capital (sources of funds)
Reserves: cash and deposits at the FedDeposits: checking, savings, time deposits
Securities: Treasuries, agency mortgage-backed securities, municipal bondsBorrowings: fed funds, repo, Federal Home Loan Bank advances, bonds
Loans: business, real estate, consumer (the main earning asset)Bank capital (equity): assets minus liabilities, the cushion against losses

Banks profit from the spread between what they earn on assets and what they pay on liabilities, plus fees. A T-account tracks changes: when you deposit a $100 check, the bank's deposits rise by $100 and, once the check clears, its reserves rise by $100.

Four management problems

  1. Liquidity management: keep enough liquid assets to meet withdrawals without fire-selling loans.
  2. Asset management: screen borrowers, diversify, and balance yield against risk.
  3. Liability management: raise funds cheaply and stably. Uninsured, flighty funding is risky.
  4. Capital adequacy: hold enough equity to absorb losses and meet regulatory minimums.

Capital, leverage and returns

ROA = net income ÷ assets  ·  ROE = net income ÷ equity  ·  ROE = ROA × (assets ÷ equity)

The ratio assets ÷ equity is leverage (the equity multiplier). With 10% capital, leverage is 10: a 1% ROA becomes a 10% ROE. Owners like leverage because it boosts returns; regulators limit it because it magnifies losses. With 5% capital, a 5% fall in asset values wipes the bank out. After 2008 the Basel III rules raised minimum capital and added liquidity requirements.

Key takeaways

  • Banks borrow short and lend long, profiting from the spread.
  • They must manage liquidity, credit risk, funding and capital.
  • ROE = ROA × leverage; leverage boosts profits and magnifies losses.

Module 8

Bank Runs, Regulation and Financial Crises

You will learn to

  • Explain the Diamond–Dybvig model of bank runs
  • Describe deposit insurance and the lender of last resort, and their moral hazard
  • Summarize Bernanke's account of the Great Depression
  • Analyze the 2008 crisis and the 2023 run on Silicon Valley Bank

The same feature that makes banks useful, turning long-term loans into deposits you can withdraw any time, makes them fragile.

The Diamond–Dybvig model

In 1983 Douglas Diamond and Philip Dybvig (both Yale PhDs, Nobel 2022) built the classic model. Households don't know when they'll need cash. Long-term projects pay well only if they run to completion; cutting them short gives poor returns. Banks resolve this by taking deposits, lending long, and letting depositors withdraw on demand: maturity transformation, which creates liquidity. But because deposits are short and loans are long, a bank can't pay everyone at once. If depositors fear others will withdraw, each one's best move is to withdraw first. A rumor alone can trigger a bank run that forces fire sales and destroys a sound bank. The run is a self-fulfilling prophecy.

Lab 8.1A Diamond–Dybvig bank run

100 depositors each put in $1. A quarter genuinely need cash early. The bank promises $1.10 on demand. Its long-term loans pay $1.50 per dollar if held to maturity but only $0.80 if liquidated early. What if some patient depositors panic?

Stopping runs

Deposit insurance removes the reason to run: if your money is guaranteed, you don't care what others do, so the insurance rarely needs to be paid out. The FDIC (created in 1933) insures deposits up to $250,000 per depositor per bank. A lender of last resort, the central bank lending freely to solvent banks against good collateral at a penalty rate, as Walter Bagehot advised in 1873, stops liquidity panics. The cost is moral hazard: protected banks and depositors take more risk, and banks seen as "too big to fail" expect rescue. Hence regulation: capital requirements, liquidity rules, supervision and stress tests.

Bernanke and the Great Depression

Between 1930 and 1933, U.S. industrial production fell 46% and unemployment reached 25%. Waves of runs cut the number of U.S. banks roughly in half in three years. Ben Bernanke (then a young Stanford professor, later at Princeton and Fed chair, Nobel 2022) showed in 1983 that bank failures were not just a symptom. When banks failed, their knowledge of borrowers and lending relationships vanished, so credit stopped flowing to sound households and firms, and rebuilding that took years. This "credit channel" explains why the Depression was so deep and long.

The 2007–09 crisis

A credit-fueled housing bubble burst in 2006. Losses on subprime mortgages, packaged into complex securities, spread through a shadow banking system of investment banks, money-market funds and repo lenders that funded long-term assets with short-term borrowing but had no deposit insurance. Yale's Gary Gorton described the panic as a run on repo: lenders demanded more collateral or stopped lending, forcing fire sales. Lehman Brothers failed in September 2008. As Fed chair, Bernanke applied his research: the Fed lent massively as lender of last resort, extended lending to non-banks, cut rates to near zero and began buying bonds. The Dodd–Frank Act (2010) followed.

Silicon Valley Bank, 2023

SVB had grown rapidly on deposits from tech firms and invested much of them in long-term bonds when rates were low. As rates rose in 2022, those bonds lost value (Module 3). When SVB announced a loss on bond sales and a plan to raise capital, depositors withdrew $42 billion in a single day, March 9, 2023, and the bank failed the next day. About 86% of its roughly $175 billion of deposits were uninsured, so depositors had every Diamond–Dybvig reason to run, and social media spread the panic in hours. Regulators then guaranteed all SVB deposits to prevent contagion.

Key takeaways

  • Maturity transformation creates liquidity but invites self-fulfilling runs.
  • Deposit insurance and a lender of last resort stop runs, at the cost of moral hazard, so regulation is needed.
  • Bank failures deepen downturns by cutting off credit (Bernanke); 2008 and 2023 were modern runs.

Module 9

Central Banks and the Federal Reserve

You will learn to

  • Explain why central banks exist
  • Describe the Fed's history and structure
  • Explain the case for central-bank independence
  • Compare the Fed with other central banks

A central bank is the bank for banks and the government, and the manager of the nation's money.

Why central banks?

In his 2012 lectures, Ben Bernanke described two core missions: macroeconomic stability (stable prices and full employment) and financial stability (preventing and managing panics, including acting as lender of last resort). The U.S. tried life without a central bank for most of the 1800s and suffered repeated panics. After the Panic of 1907, which J. P. Morgan personally helped contain, Congress created the Federal Reserve in 1913.

How the Fed is organized

PartRole
Board of Governors (Washington)Seven governors appointed by the President and confirmed by the Senate for 14-year terms. The Chair serves a 4-year term.
12 regional Federal Reserve BanksSupervise banks, run payments, research their districts. The New York Fed conducts market operations.
Federal Open Market Committee (FOMC)The 7 governors plus the New York Fed president and 4 rotating Reserve Bank presidents: 12 votes. It sets monetary policy at eight scheduled meetings a year.

Congress gave the Fed a dual mandate: maximum employment and stable prices, interpreted since 2012 as 2% inflation measured by the PCE price index.

Independence

Politicians face elections and are tempted to stimulate the economy before votes, or to have the central bank finance deficits, both recipes for inflation. Studies across countries find that more independent central banks deliver lower inflation without lower growth. Independence comes with accountability: testimony to Congress, published statements, minutes, projections, and a chair's press conference after each meeting. The Fed is independent within government, not of it: Congress can change its powers.

The Fed today

Kevin Warsh chairs the Fed. In September 2026 the FOMC raised its target range for the federal funds rate by a quarter point, to 3.75%–4.00%, its first increase since 2023, and on October 28, 2026 it held the rate there by a 10–2 vote. Warsh has said one 2027 meeting will focus entirely on policy strategy, with no rate vote expected. Inflation was still above target: the Fed's staff estimated PCE inflation at 3.8% over the year to August 2026, and unemployment was 4.1%.

Other central banks

The European Central Bank (1998) sets policy for the euro area with a primary mandate of price stability. The Bank of England (1694) gained operational independence in 1997 with an inflation target. The Bank of Japan pioneered quantitative easing in the 2000s. Many central banks use inflation targeting: an announced numerical goal plus transparent communication.

Key takeaways

  • Central banks pursue price stability, employment and financial stability.
  • The Fed is a federal structure: a Board, 12 Reserve Banks, and the FOMC that sets policy.
  • Independence plus accountability delivers lower inflation.

Module 10

The Money Supply Process

You will learn to

  • Read the Fed's balance sheet and define the monetary base
  • Show how banks create deposits by lending
  • Derive the money multiplier and what changes it
  • Explain why the multiplier stopped describing reality after 2008

Most money is created not by the central bank but by commercial banks when they make loans.

The Fed's balance sheet

Fed assetsFed liabilities
Treasury securitiesCurrency in circulation
Agency mortgage-backed securitiesBank reserves (deposits banks hold at the Fed)
Loans to banks (discount window)Treasury's account, reverse repos

The monetary base = currency + reserves. When the Fed buys a bond from a bank (an open market purchase), it pays by crediting the bank's reserve account: the base rises. Sales do the reverse.

How banks create money

When a bank makes a loan, it credits the borrower's account with a new deposit, and deposits are money. As the borrower spends, the deposit moves to other banks, which can lend again. In the textbook model, with a required reserve ratio rr, a currency ratio c (currency held per dollar of deposits) and an excess reserve ratio e:

Money supply M = m × MB, where m = (1 + c) ÷ (rr + e + c)With rr = 10%, e = 0 and c = 0, $1 of new reserves supports $10 of deposits.
Lab 10.1The money multiplier

The monetary base is $1 trillion. Change the required reserve ratio, how much cash the public holds, and how many extra reserves banks keep.

Why the multiplier broke

The multiplier treats banks as lending out every spare dollar of reserves. After 2008, the Fed's bond purchases (quantitative easing) multiplied the monetary base several times over, yet broad money grew far less, because banks simply held the new reserves. e soared and the multiplier collapsed. Since 2008 the Fed has paid interest on reserves, making them an attractive asset, and in March 2020 it cut reserve requirements to zero. Today banks' lending is limited by profitable opportunities, capital rules and demand for loans, not by reserves. As the Bank of England put it in a well-known 2014 explainer, loans create deposits, not the other way round. The multiplier is still a useful accounting identity, but not a causal machine.

Key takeaways

  • Monetary base = currency + reserves; open market operations change it.
  • Bank lending creates deposits; the multiplier m = (1 + c)/(rr + e + c) summarizes the link.
  • With ample reserves paying interest and zero reserve requirements, the multiplier no longer constrains money creation.

Module 11

Monetary Policy: Tools and Strategy

You will learn to

  • Explain how the Fed sets interest rates with ample reserves
  • Describe quantitative easing and tightening, and forward guidance
  • Use the Taylor rule
  • Trace how policy reaches the economy

The Fed doesn't decree the interest rate. It sets the terms on which banks can lend to it and borrow from it, and markets follow.

Setting rates with ample reserves

The FOMC targets the federal funds rate, the overnight rate at which banks lend reserves to each other, within a range a quarter point wide. Before 2008, reserves were scarce and the New York Fed nudged the rate by adding or draining small amounts. Now the system has ample reserves and the Fed steers the rate with administered rates:

ToolHow it worksSetting after Sept 2026
Interest on reserve balances (IORB)No bank lends reserves for much less than it earns by leaving them at the Fed3.90%
Overnight reverse repo (ON RRP)Money funds and others can park cash at the Fed, setting a floor under market rates3.75%
Discount rate (primary credit) and standing repo facilityBanks can always borrow from the Fed at this rate, putting a ceiling on market rates4.00%

When the FOMC raises its target, it raises all three together. The quantity of reserves barely matters, as long as it stays "ample."

Balance-sheet policy and guidance

When the policy rate hits zero, central banks use quantitative easing (QE): large-scale purchases of long-term bonds to push down long-term rates. The Fed did this after 2008 and in 2020. Quantitative tightening (QT) shrinks holdings by letting bonds mature without replacement. As of late 2026 the Fed is rolling over maturing Treasuries and buying Treasury bills as needed to keep reserves ample. Forward guidance (signaling the future path of rates) works through expectations, since long rates reflect expected short rates (Module 5).

The Taylor rule

John Taylor (Stanford) proposed in 1993 that policy follows a simple rule:

i = r* + π + 0.5 (π − 2) + 0.5 × (output gap)r* is the neutral real rate and π is inflation. Raising the rate more than one-for-one with inflation (the "Taylor principle") lifts the real rate when inflation rises.
Lab 11.2The Taylor rule

Set inflation and the output gap. The rule's prescribed rate is compared with the actual Fed funds target (3.75–4.00% as of November 2026).

Transmission

Policy reaches the economy through several channels: interest rates (borrowing costs for homes, cars and investment), asset prices (stocks and houses, and therefore wealth), the exchange rate (higher rates strengthen the dollar and reduce net exports), credit (bank lending and borrowers' balance sheets, Bernanke's credit channel), and expectations. Effects arrive with "long and variable lags," as Friedman warned, so policy must look ahead.

Key takeaways

  • With ample reserves, IORB, ON RRP and the discount rate steer the fed funds rate.
  • QE, QT and forward guidance work on long rates and expectations.
  • The Taylor rule links the policy rate to inflation and the output gap.

Module 12

Money, Inflation and the International System

You will learn to

  • Use the quantity theory of money
  • Explain why big inflations are monetary, and how they end
  • Apply interest parity to exchange rates
  • Explain the international monetary trilemma

"Inflation is always and everywhere a monetary phenomenon." Friedman's line is right about the long run. Understanding why is the last piece of the puzzle.

The quantity theory

M × V = P × YMoney × velocity = price level × real output. In growth rates: inflation ≈ money growth + velocity growth − real output growth.

If velocity is stable and output grows at its own pace, faster money growth shows up mostly as inflation. Friedman and Anna Schwartz's A Monetary History of the United States (1963) documented this over a century, and argued that the Fed's failure to stop the money supply from collapsing turned the 1929 downturn into the Great Depression. In the short run velocity is unstable (it plunged in 2020), so the link is loose year to year but strong over decades and across countries.

Lab 12.1The quantity theory in growth rates

Choose money growth, velocity growth and real output growth. Inflation is what's left over.

How big inflations end

Thomas Sargent (Princeton/NYU, Nobel 2011) studied four post-WWI hyperinflations in Austria, Hungary, Germany and Poland. Each ended abruptly, not through slow money tightening, but when a credible change of regime stopped deficits being financed by printing money: an independent central bank and a balanced budget. Expectations turned on a dime. The lesson: credibility and the fiscal backdrop matter as much as the money supply.

Exchange rates and interest parity

Capital moves to where expected returns are highest. With free capital flows, interest parity holds: the gap between two countries' interest rates equals the expected change in their exchange rate. If dollar rates are 2 points above euro rates, investors must expect the dollar to depreciate about 2% a year; otherwise everyone would pile into dollars. A central bank that raises rates relative to the rest of the world tends to strengthen its currency today.

The trilemma

Robert Mundell (Columbia, Nobel 1999) and Marcus Fleming showed that a country cannot have all three at once: a fixed exchange rate, free capital movement, and an independent monetary policy. It must pick two.

Fixed rate + free capitalGives up monetary independence: Hong Kong's dollar peg, euro-area members.
Free capital + independent policyMust let the currency float: the U.S., U.K. and Japan.
Fixed rate + independent policyRequires capital controls: the Bretton Woods system (1944–71), China for many years.
HistoryThe gold standard chose fixed rates and free capital; Bretton Woods used controls; since 1973 major economies float.

At the top of today's monetary hierarchy (Module 1) sits the U.S. dollar. During global crises the Fed lends dollars to other central banks through swap lines, acting as a lender of last resort to the world, as it did in 2008 and 2020.

Key takeaways

  • Inflation ≈ money growth + velocity growth − output growth; over long periods, money growth drives inflation.
  • Ending high inflation takes a credible regime change.
  • Interest parity links rates and exchange rates; the trilemma forces a choice of two out of three.

  • R. Mundell, "Capital Mobility and Stabilization Policy under Fixed and Flexible Exchange Rates" (1963)
